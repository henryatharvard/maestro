## 10. Evidence and evaluation (E)

Every design claim made so far in this review — that infilling preserves ownership, that annotations beat prompts, that composers want to stay in notation — is either an empirical claim or an aesthetic preference dressed as one. This section collects the evidence that lets the distinction be drawn: controlled studies of co-creative music systems (E1), field studies of practitioners (E2), deployed systems with published usage data (E3), metrics for generated music (E4), creativity-evaluation frameworks (E5), and benchmarks probing whether models know any music theory (E6); it closes with a concrete evaluation protocol. The organising finding is a mismatch. The generative-model literature is measured almost entirely with distributional audio metrics that correlate weakly with human judgement, while the interaction literature — which measures what the studio actually claims to improve — consists of a few dozen studies with median N in the low teens, one four-week field study, and one deployed system with telemetry.

### 10.1 E1 Controlled studies of co-creative music systems

The most load-bearing study here is Cococo [@louie2020cococo], because it isolates the interface from the model. Louie, Coenen, Huang, Terry and Cai built a four-voice editor over Coconet [@huang2017coconet] with four AI-steering tools — voice lanes restricting generation to chosen voices and time spans, semantic sliders (conventional↔surprising, happy↔sad), an example-based similarity slider, and multiple alternatives for audition — all implemented as soft priors on the sampling distribution rather than by retraining. A within-subjects study with twenty-one novice composers (two 15-minute pieces, one per interface), preceded by a formative study with eleven, compared Cococo against a conventional generate-and-accept infilling interface. On seven-point scales Cococo scored higher on controllability (5.9 vs. 3.5), comprehensibility (5.3 vs. 3.2), self-efficacy (5.9 vs. 3.7), sense of collaboration (5.9 vs. 4.0), creative expression (5.5 vs. 3.8) and ownership (5.2 vs. 3.8), all at p<0.01. Participants built pieces bit-by-bit, voice by voice, in generate–audition–edit loops rather than accepting whole outputs, and developed explicit strategies for *debugging* the model. The authors' conclusion — that generative capability "may need to be partitioned into smaller, semantically meaningful tools" — is the closest thing in this literature to a specification for an annotation layer.

Expressive Communication [@louie2022expressive] asked whether interface work survives model improvement. Its 2×2 design crossed model (PerformanceRNN vs. Music Transformer [@huang2018musictransformer]) with interface (a "Radio" condition curating ten randomly generated whole phrases vs. a "Steering" condition permitting chunk-by-chunk composition with semantic filters), in a task requiring composers to communicate a given card's feeling. Twenty-six composers aged 24–60 produced over 100 pieces, and twenty listeners made 1,020 head-to-head comparisons. Both factors mattered and neither dominated: the better model produced pieces that better evoked the target feeling (p<0.001) and sounded more musical (p<1e-5), while the steering interface also improved evocation and decisively raised ownership (4.8 vs. 2.42) and control (5.0 vs. 2.58) at p<1e-6. Steering mattered most when the target emotion fought the model's prior — that is, it compensated for model bias. The authors' formulation, that "more expressive models and more steerable interfaces are important and complementary," is the warrant for building interaction machinery during rapid model progress; the study's structure (composer self-report plus blind listener judgement of communicative intent) is a reusable protocol.

Amuse [@kim2025amuse] supplies the equivalent evidence for multimodal annotation. Its within-subjects study compared Amuse-plus-Aria against Aria alone [@donahue2024hookpadaria] with ten songwriters (eight hobbyist, two professional) writing an eight-bar chorus in 25 minutes, analysed by Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Inspiration support rose from 4.60 to 6.20 (p<0.01) and task alignment from 4.30 to 6.10 (p=0.016); the CSI facets of controllability (4.40→5.80), collaboration (3.90→5.80), exploration (4.40→6.10) and expressiveness (4.20→6.00) all improved at p<0.05. Two nulls matter as much: completion time was unchanged (17.4 vs. 17.6 minutes) and externally judged quality did not differ, though *self-rated* quality rose (5.30→6.40, p=0.023). A listening study with forty-five raters and 900 pairwise judgements found Amuse's progressions on a par with an LSTM harmonic prior for coherence, ahead of raw GPT-4o (p = .009) and most preferred for keyword relevance in 58% of judgements. The honest reading is that multimodal inspiration changes the *experience* of composing measurably and the *artefact* not at all.

The MMM-C study [@tchemeube2023mmmc] quantifies the cost of the opposite design choice. Integrating the Multi-Track Music Machine [@ens2020mmm; @tchemeube2022calliope] into Cubase as a plugin whose only exposed parameter was temperature, Tchemeube and colleagues ran a three-part mixed-methods study with eighteen expert composers (eight hobbyist, ten professional). Usability was acceptable (SUS 71.4–75.7 across three tasks) and acceptance moderate (TAM ease 3.1–3.3/5, usefulness 3.4–3.7/5), CSI enjoyment strongest at 3.85/5. But ease of control scored 5.2/10 against a *desire for more control* of 9.5/10 — the largest measured control deficit in this literature, and a direct measurement of what an under-parameterised compile step feels like. Since the model was strong and the DAW integration real, the complaint is squarely about the control channel.

Smaller studies triangulate individual primitives. ChordRipple's chord-substitution study found adventurous recommendations significantly increased chord novelty (p=0.0039) [@huang2016chordripple]. Drawlody [@liang2024drawlody] evaluated sketch-to-melody with eighteen participants (six musically trained), reporting user-friendliness of 4.38/5 against 2.94 for a chord-based interface and 2.38 for a DAW, and interpretability of 4.50/5 — notable because that score attaches to a visualisation of *how the sketch was mapped to the melody*, i.e. to feedback about the compile step rather than output quality. RefinPaint [@ramoneda2024refinpaint] ran a listening study with fifteen annotators across fragment sizes of 50%, 30% and 10%, finding its critic-plus-inpainter loop preferred at all sizes, with four amateur composers reporting improved drafts. Composer's Assistant 2 [@malandro2024composersassistant2] found no quality difference between real music and pieces co-composed with its infilling controls. Loop Copilot [@zhang2023loopcopilot] evaluated conversational orchestration of audio backends with eight users (SUS 75.3±15.3, TAM 4.09±1.09), drawing the complaints that recur throughout: limited attribute control, unpredictable prompt responsiveness, no DAW integration. MultiVerse [@wang2026multiverse] compared creator-authored constraints against prompting with ten songwriters, who preferred explicit constraint authoring despite its cost in iteration speed. ComposerX had 32.2% of its outputs judged human-composed against 55.4% for genuine human pieces, without reporting rater counts [@deng2024composerx], and MIDI-LLM's in-the-wild deployment covered fifty-eight musicians and roughly 4,000 generations, with lead-sheet generation most accepted [@wu2025midillm].

Music Creation by Example [@frid2020example] found, across 104 video creators, that example-based input is more natural than tag or text search for people who think in references rather than theory terms. Two studies go beyond turn-taking. ReaLJam [@scarlatos2025realjam], built over the RL-tuned ReaLchords accompanist [@wu2024realchords], ran six experienced pianists through hour-long sessions across a baseline plus eight modified settings. RL-tuned agents beat the pre-trained baseline on all measures; enjoyment averaged 4.3/5 but perceived adaptation only 2.7/5; five of six wanted the agent's upcoming chords displayed before commitment; and — the finding most relevant to shipping defaults — preferences across the other settings varied so widely between individuals that no configuration won. AI as Social Glue [@suh2021socialglue] studied thirty participants in fifteen stratified pairs co-composing with and without Cococo's model, and found it acted as a *role transformer*: pairs drifted from co-composing to co-*producing*, evaluating output rather than authoring material.

The only longitudinal study in this cluster is Rhapsody Refiner [@krol2025ownership], a tool deliberately unable to compose from scratch: it masks notes in the musician's own MIDI and re-predicts them with MusicBERT [@zeng2021musicbert] under user-set parameters. Eight practising musicians used it for four weeks with journals, interaction logs and interviews, analysed thematically. Participants valued it for "moments, not whole ideas," rejected automatic completion, and reported that its dependence on their own input produced ownership of both process and artefact; imperfection prompted problem-solving rather than frustration. This is the strongest available evidence for the studio's central bet, and a demonstration of the study design the field lacks.

Against all of this sits the CMU experiment by Oros, Telang and Randall [@oros2026cmu], the field's sharpest cautionary result and its weakest-documented one. In a randomised design, 140 musically trained participants each composed a 15-second melody on a small keyboard; a randomly assigned group could use Udio [@udio2025] to generate audio "for inspiration," and separate listeners rated all melodies on creativity, enjoyment and musicality. As reported, AI-assisted melodies were slower, used fewer notes and were judged less creative, and human-only melodies outperformed the AI inspirations themselves. These claims need care: as of September 2026 the work exists only as a conference poster (November 2025) and a university news story (January 2026), with no public paper, no effect sizes, no rater counts, and no disambiguation of "slower" (tempo or composition time); the related SSRN working paper "Help That Hurts" [@oros2026helpthathurts] was verified only through its Crossref record. Taken as indicative, the study says that bolting a prompt-to-audio generator beside a composition task can degrade trained composers' output. It says nothing about an integrated symbolic co-creator, which is why the studio should be evaluated with the same randomised, blind-rater design rather than cite the result rhetorically.

### 10.2 E2 Field studies and practitioner perspectives

The requirements literature here is qualitative and remarkably consistent. Its founding document is the AI Song Contest study [@huang2020aisongcontest], a thematic analysis of the thirteen teams (61 people, 2–15 per team, median 4) that entered the 2020 contest. Teams used a median of four model types and "rarely attempted end-to-end generation"; instead they *decomposed* songwriting into melody, harmony, bass, drums, lyrics and structure, generated massively (one team 450 melodies, another 10,000 lyric lines) and curated post hoc because the models were not steerable, authoring structure by hand because small models lacked holistic context. The call for interfaces that are "decomposable, steerable, interpretable, and adaptive" is a requirements list written by practitioners before the tools existed. HAISP [@morris2024haisp] turned that into a resource, coding the *process* descriptions contest teams submitted — which tools for which task, what control creators retained — from 34 submissions in 2023 and, in 2025, the 2024 cohort, whose comparison surfaced differences in creative agency between general-purpose and fine-tuned custom tools.

I Keep Counting [@micchi2021ikeepcounting] documents the first-person counterpart, logging more than twenty-five human interventions in one contest entry and arguing that deliberate selection and modification of AI material constitutes authorship. The deepest professional study remains Sony CSL's [@deruty2022sonycsl], a reflection on 6–18-month collaborations with six professional acts using in-house prototypes. Its findings read as design constraints: practitioners want "pull" (explicit queries) not "push" (unsolicited suggestions); they want the system *primed on their own audio*; they generate and curate, navigate latent spaces, and exploit artefacts as aesthetic material. The frictions named are leaving the DAW, the absence of recognisable controls, and the lack of visualisation. The paper also mounts the strongest challenge to a symbolic-first studio, arguing that in-studio popular-music practice is audio-first and that audio tools fit it better than MIDI tools — a claim to answer with rendering and stems rather than dismiss (see S3.8). Newman, Morris and Lee [@newman2023perceptions] interviewed six professional creators for 60–90 minutes each with two-coder analysis; their design implications nearly restate the studio's stance: confine AI to ideation rather than intentional decisions; provide interoperability through MIDI, WAV and *MusicXML* export or in-software integration; separate "influences" (ideas) from "mechanisms" (housekeeping); and prefer open-source, explainable models to black boxes.

Complaints about text-to-audio tools are now quantified. Ronchini and colleagues [@ronchini2025ttmuserstudy] gave seventeen producers from seven countries an hour with a custom interface pairing MusicGen [@copet2023musicgen] with HT-Demucs stem separation. Text-to-music was seen as an ideation aid, not production-ready: 94% would use it in ideation, while the barriers were intent–output misalignment, failure to align to a project's tempo, key and beat grid, and limited editability, plus ethical concerns about copyright, compensation and homogenisation from Western-centric training data. Fu and colleagues [@fu2025novice] followed three teams of novice producers through a ten-week course (nine of twenty interviewed) and identified an emergent stage they call "collaging and refinement" — the labour of integrating heterogeneous AI outputs into a coherent track, precisely the assembly step this review calls *compile*, and one no tool supports. Ford and colleagues [@ford2024reflection] had eight composer-researchers each compose with a different AI tool while pausing hourly to reflect on screenshots, and found reflection clustered around *curation*: decisions concentrate where alternatives are compared, which argues for surfacing history and alternatives, not only generation (see S4.5). Cavez and colleagues' interviews with nine professional composers [@cavez2024challenges] analysed notation software through the Cognitive Dimensions of Notations, documenting premature commitment, viscosity and continued reliance on paper — justification for a loosely structured annotation layer over a strict symbolic model, and the motivation for their EuterPen prototype [@cavez2025euterpen] (cross-ref S1.3).

Sturm, Ben-Tal and colleagues' folk-rnn case study [@sturm2019machinefolk] tracked an ABC-notation model through years of use by traditional and contemporary musicians, in concerts, an album and a musical, concluding that generated tunes are *raw material* requiring editing, arrangement and performance, and that the relevant evaluation is usefulness to practice rather than likelihood; its companion paper [@sturm2019openquestions] adds the engineer's duty to document limitations and evaluate for fairness and transparency, not accuracy alone. An early wave of studies on generative AI in composition *teaching* is also appearing [@oros2026helpthathurts], whose "help that hurts" finding — AI ideation reducing the quality and originality of student composition output — is plausibly the same experiment written up, since it shares the authors, the finding and the period; it should not be read as independent corroboration of the CMU poster. But these papers were verified only through Crossref records, appear in venues of mixed quality, and their designs could not be checked; treat them as signals worth replicating, not results.

### 10.3 E3 Deployed systems with usage data

Only four systems in this review report deployment usage data. Hookpad Aria [@donahue2024hookpadaria] is the most directly relevant: a copilot inside a web lead-sheet editor supporting continuation, infilling of spans in the middle of existing material, and harmony-from-melody or melody-from-harmony, built on anticipatory conditioning [@thickstun2024anticipatory]. From its March 2024 release it had generated 318,000 suggestions for 3,000 users, of which 74,000 were accepted into songs — roughly 23% acceptance over the reporting period to early 2025. This is the best public evidence that span-level suggestions inside a notation editor are wanted and used, and it makes acceptance rate a cheap, ecologically valid signal — the authors' "data flywheel for music co-creation." The Bach Doodle [@huang2019bachdoodle] answers the scale question: users entered two-bar melodies on a simplified staff and Coconet harmonised them in four voices in about two seconds in-browser, drawing over 55 million queries in three days. Its by-product is the more durable contribution — a CC BY 4.0 dataset of 21.6 million harmonisations from 8.5 million sessions, with melody, harmonisation, user rating and playback count: a unique record of how people rate AI harmonisations of their own material. FlowComposer [@papadopoulos2016flowcomposer] contributes the oldest and most actionable professional usage observation: composers using its constrained lead-sheet regeneration rarely generated more than eight bars at a time, using it for fragments inside longer human compositions. Casini and colleagues [@casini2025sunoudio] provide the negative image, clustering text embeddings of user prompts, metatags and generated lyrics from Suno and Udio over May–October 2024; the resulting map of how people try to steer a text-to-song system — including the peculiar metatag strategies users invent to obtain control the interface withholds — catalogues the pain a symbolic annotation layer is meant to remove.

### 10.4 E4 Metrics for generated music

Automatic metrics for generated music are, on current evidence, adequate for regression testing and inadequate for judging quality. The default has been Fréchet Audio Distance [@kilgour2019fad], a Fréchet distance between Gaussians fitted to VGGish embeddings of reference and generated audio — proposed for music *enhancement* and validated only against ratings of enhancement artefacts, then adopted wholesale for generative evaluation. Gui and colleagues [@gui2024fad] documented its failure modes — sample-size bias, VGGish as a poor embedding, weak reference sets — and prescribed a better recipe: extrapolate to infinite sample size, use CLAP-LAION-music embeddings and music-specific references, and compute per-song FAD for outlier detection. KAD [@chung2025kad] abandons the Gaussian assumption entirely for an MMD kernel distance that is distribution-free, unbiased, convergent at small sample sizes and GPU-scalable, reporting stronger alignment with human perception. Fréchet Music Distance [@retkowski2024fmd] is the first widely reported distributional metric over *symbolic* music in this corpus. More useful for a compiler are Yang and Lerch's interpretable symbolic features [@yang2020evaluation] — pitch-class histograms and transition matrices, note-length histograms, inter-onset intervals — compared as distributions against a reference set via KL divergence and overlap area, and released as mgeval. These are metrics a composer can read, which suits them to compile-time diagnostics rather than scoring.

For text-conditioned output the standard adherence measure is the CLAP score [@wu2023clap], a cosine similarity between prompt and audio embeddings, evaluated against caption corpora: MusicCaps' 5,500 expert-captioned ten-second clips [@agostinelli2023musiclm] and the Song Describer Dataset's 1.1k crowd captions over 706 CC-licensed tracks [@manco2023songdescriber], the latter created partly because MusicCaps is YouTube-derived and licence-constrained. MusicEval [@liu2025musiceval] supplies expert reference ratings — 2,748 clips from 31 systems over 384 prompts, rated by fourteen professionals for 13,740 ratings on overall impression and text alignment — plus a CLAP-based MOS-predictor baseline, and became the basis of AudioMOS Challenge 2025 Track 1 [@huang2025audiomos]. That challenge (24 teams) and the ICASSP 2026 Automatic Song Aesthetics Evaluation challenge established MOS prediction as a research line, including Audiobox Aesthetics' four axes (production quality, complexity, enjoyment, usefulness). Such predictors are pre-filters that rank candidate renders before the composer hears them — useful inside a compile loop, worthless as a claim about the work.

The strongest results here measure the metrics themselves. Music Arena [@kim2025musicarena] runs live pairwise human-preference evaluation of text-to-music models with LLM-based routing across heterogeneous model signatures, collects listening behaviour and free-text feedback, and releases rolling open data — infrastructure the studio could reuse. MAD [@huang2025aligning] showed FAD to be inconsistent and weakly correlated with human judgement, proposed MAUVE Audio Divergence over self-supervised embeddings, and released the MusicPrefs dataset; MAD reached an average rank correlation of 0.84 against FAD's 0.49 on synthetic tests, and 0.62 against FAD's 0.14 with MusicPrefs. Grötschla and colleagues [@grotschla2025benchmarking] gathered 15,000 pairwise comparisons from 2,500 raters over 6,000 songs from twelve state-of-the-art generators, producing the first large human-preference ranking and documenting how weakly FAD, KAD, CLAP and related metrics track it; Zhang and colleagues [@zhang2025aesthetics] independently found aesthetic and distributional metrics inconsistent with human preference across five leading systems. The survey by Kader and Karmaker [@kader2025survey] names poor metric–perception correlation, cross-cultural bias and absent standardisation as the open problems. The conclusion the studio should adopt explicitly: distributional audio metrics are sanity checks — did this build regress? — and never evidence that a co-creative tool works.

Three further instruments serve purposes a co-creative studio needs. MiRA [@batlleroca2024mira] combines audio-similarity metrics (CLAP, Discogs-EffNet, cover-identification embeddings) to detect replication of training data in generated audio above roughly 10% — the open baseline for auditing any renderer the studio ships, alongside work on whether watermarks in training data survive into outputs [@epple2024watermarking]. Echoes of Humanity [@figueiredo2025echoes] contributes a template for listener discrimination: a blind, Turing-like pairwise test with randomised crossover over real-world Suno outputs and human songs, controlling pairwise similarity. Its causal finding — that discrimination *rises* when the paired items are similar, and that the cues cited are mostly vocal and technical — means any "can listeners tell compiled from hand-arranged" test must control pair matching or it measures nothing. At population scale, the Deezer/Ipsos survey [@deezer2025ipsos] of 9,000 adults across eight countries found 97% failed to distinguish fully AI-generated tracks blind, while 80% wanted labelling — undercutting "listeners cannot tell" as a design goal and supporting provenance metadata as a feature (see C3).

### 10.5 E5 Creativity and co-creativity evaluation frameworks

The instruments for measuring creative *support* predate music AI and are better developed than the metrics above. The Creativity Support Index [@cherry2014csi] is the workhorse: a validated survey scoring a tool on six factors — Collaboration, Enjoyment, Exploration, Expressiveness, Immersion, Results Worth Effort — from paired agreement items, with paired-factor comparisons weighting each by task importance to yield a 0–100 score. It is used, whole or by facet, across this literature [@louie2020cococo; @tchemeube2023mmmc; @kim2025amuse], making per-facet reporting the pragmatic choice. Karimi and colleagues [@karimi2018evaluating] supply what CSI alone misses: four questions — *who* evaluates, *what* is evaluated (product, process, interaction, user experience), *when* and *how* — organising a survey whose central finding is a warning that co-creative systems overwhelmingly evaluate user experience and under-measure the creativity of the collaboration or of the artefact. For the studio that means measuring how annotations change compiled outputs, not only how composers feel about annotating.

The computational-creativity tradition contributes complementary standards. Jordanous's SPECS [@jordanous2012standardised] prescribes defining creativity for the domain from fourteen empirically derived components — originality, value, intention and emotional involvement, domain competence, variety — before testing against it. Ritchie's eighteen criteria [@ritchie2007criteria] formalise the relation between an "inspiring set" and an output set using ratings of typicality and quality — the structure of a studio compile, where the composer's material is the inspiring set and the question is whether outputs are typical of it and good without being copies. Colton's creative tripod [@colton2008tripod] holds that a system is perceived as creative when it exhibits skill, appreciation and imagination; *appreciation* — evaluating one's own output against the composer's stated constraints — is the missing leg in nearly every generator surveyed here and a design goal for the compiler's critic. Boden's distinctions [@boden2004creativemind] fix the vocabulary: psychological versus historical creativity, and combinational, exploratory and transformational creativity. In those terms the studio's claim is modest and testable — the compiler performs *exploratory* generation inside a space the composer defines and transforms. Kantosalo and Toivonen's modes [@kantosalo2016modes] give the interaction lens: the loop is *alternating* at the artefact level but *task-divided* at the role level, and the two plausibly require different measures. Finally, COFI's survey of ninety-two co-creative systems [@rezwana2023cofi] found almost no AI→human communication — both a gap and a measurement problem, since no instrument evaluates the quality of such an explanation.

### 10.6 E6 Music-theory competence benchmarks

If an LLM is to sit at the front of the compiler, interpreting textual annotations and dispatching to symbolic operators, its musical competence is a load-bearing engineering parameter. The benchmarks are discouraging. MusicTheoryBench, released with ChatMusician [@yuan2024chatmusician] — a LLaMA-2-7B continually pretrained on MusicPile's roughly 4B tokens of ABC notation and music text — tests college-level theory knowledge and reasoning by multiple choice; ChatMusician beats LLaMA-2 and GPT-3.5 zero-shot, while GPT-4's accuracy on the reasoning split is reported near random (a figure recalled rather than re-verified). ZIQI-Eval [@li2024ziqieval] scales the knowledge question to over 14,000 questions in ten categories and fifty-six sub-categories, reporting all sixteen LLMs tested as poor. ABC-Eval [@zhao2025abceval] localises the failure: across 1,086 items in ten sub-tasks over ABC scores, seven models including GPT-5, Gemini and DeepSeek exceed 90% on syntax while scoring near random on sequence-level tasks such as emotion — they parse the notation and cannot hear it. WildScore [@mundada2025wildscore] extends the question to multimodal reasoning over real score images, finding clear gaps.

The audio side is no better grounded. MuChoMusic [@weck2024muchomusic] built 1,187 human-validated multiple-choice questions over 644 tracks from MusicCaps and the Song Describer Dataset, covering theory and instrumentation knowledge as well as cultural and functional reasoning, and found five open audio-language models over-relying on the language modality — answering plausibly without really listening. That matters for any pipeline where an audio-LLM interprets a hummed phrase or a pasted reference recording as an annotation. SynTheory [@wei2024musictheory] asks whether generative backbones encode theory at all, using a synthetic MIDI-and-audio dataset isolating tempo, time signature, notes, intervals, scales, chords and progressions and probing Jukebox and MusicGen layer by layer: the concepts are linearly detectable, with detectability varying by model size and layer — a usable diagnostic before trusting a backbone with an annotation vocabulary. Controlled comparisons of adaptation strategies show that such choices materially change outcomes and that standard metrics behave idiosyncratically for music [@kumar2026howfar], and surveys of music foundation models [@ma2024foundation] frame the conclusion architecturally: LLMs are best used as orchestrators around specialised symbolic models and deterministic analysers, with their theory claims verified rather than trusted.

### 10.7 An evaluation protocol for the studio

The literature above supports a specific, affordable instrument set; what matters is stating what each part measures and what it cannot see. The core comparative instrument should be the Creativity Support Index reported per facet rather than as a single number [@cherry2014csi], since facet-level reporting is what makes results commensurable with Cococo, MMM-C and Amuse [@louie2020cococo; @tchemeube2023mmmc; @kim2025amuse]. Alongside it belong the four constructs this literature repeatedly shows moving when control granularity changes — ownership, controllability, comprehensibility and self-efficacy — using the seven-point item wording from Cococo and Expressive Communication so effects can be compared against published baselines of 5.9 versus 3.5 for controllability and 5.2 versus 3.8 for ownership [@louie2020cococo; @louie2022expressive]. This battery measures whether an annotation layer changes experienced agency. It cannot see whether the music got better: Amuse raised self-rated quality while externally judged quality did not move [@kim2025amuse]. Self-report alone will flatter any well-designed interface.

The artefact question needs blind paired listener comparisons on a *communicative* task, following Expressive Communication: the composer is given a target to convey, listeners judge in forced-choice pairs whether the intent came across and how musical the result is, and the analysis crosses interface with model conditions so the two are not confounded [@louie2022expressive]. Two refinements are mandatory: pairs must be similarity-matched, since Echoes of Humanity showed causally that discrimination rises when items resemble each other [@figueiredo2025echoes]; and rater pools must be large, with Grötschla and colleagues' 15,000 comparisons from 2,500 raters as the scale to aspire to and Music Arena as reusable infrastructure [@grotschla2025benchmarking; @kim2025musicarena]. This measures whether compiled output communicates and pleases. It does not measure whether the composer would put their name on it, and it under-weights structure and long-range form, since paired tests use short excerpts — the weakness structural analyses identify in deep generators generally [@dai2022missing].

Deployment telemetry is the cheapest and most ecologically valid instrument, and Hookpad Aria establishes the metric: suggestions offered, suggestions accepted, the ratio [@donahue2024hookpadaria]. For an annotation-driven compiler the analogue is richer — acceptance rate per annotation type, edit distance between compiled output and what the composer kept, recompile cycles before acceptance, and the span length of accepted material, read against FlowComposer's observation that composers rarely want more than eight bars at once [@papadopoulos2016flowcomposer]. Such telemetry captures revealed preference continuously and at scale, and can seed a public dataset as the Bach Doodle's did [@huang2019bachdoodle]. Its blind spot is severe: acceptance measures local attractiveness, not whether the finished piece is good or whether the composer's voice survived, and a system that maximises acceptance can drift into the curation trap Social Glue documented [@suh2021socialglue].

That blind spot is what a longitudinal in-practice study is for, and Rhapsody Refiner's four-week ecological design — a small cohort of practising musicians, journals, interaction logs, interviews and thematic analysis [@krol2025ownership] — is the model, with Sony CSL's 6–18-month embedded collaborations as the more ambitious form [@deruty2022sonycsl]. Only this design can observe whether composers use the compiler for "moments, not whole ideas," whether ownership persists past novelty, and whether the tool changes what they write; it is also the only way to test the failure the CMU experiment gestures at under conditions where the assistance is integrated rather than bolted on [@oros2026cmu]. Four supporting instruments complete the set cheaply: interpretable symbolic diagnostics as compiler regression tests [@yang2020evaluation], with Fréchet Music Distance for distributional drift [@retkowski2024fmd]; KAD or MAD rather than raw FAD for any audio renderer, strictly as sanity checks [@chung2025kad; @huang2025aligning; @gui2024fad]; MiRA-style replication audits plus provenance logging of every human intervention as authorship evidence [@batlleroca2024mira; @micchi2021ikeepcounting]; and SynTheory-style probing with MusicTheoryBench, ABC-Eval and MuChoMusic as acceptance tests on any model considered for the compiler's front end [@wei2024musictheory; @yuan2024chatmusician; @zhao2025abceval; @weck2024muchomusic]. The plan should be written against Karimi's four questions, so the *interaction* and the *process* are evaluated and not only the experience [@karimi2018evaluating], and against SPECS' requirement that "creative support" be defined for this domain before it is measured [@jordanous2012standardised].

### 10.8 Implications for the studio

Read together, this evidence licenses three of the studio's core commitments and refuses a fourth. It licenses region-scoped, constraint-driven regeneration over whole-output generation: Cococo's ownership and control gains, Expressive Communication's finding that steering compensates for model bias, MMM-C's measured control deficit under a one-slider design, and Rhapsody Refiner's finding that transforming authored material sustains ownership all point the same way [@louie2020cococo; @louie2022expressive; @tchemeube2023mmmc; @krol2025ownership]. It licenses multimodal annotation as a steering channel, through Amuse's within-subjects gains, Drawlody's usability margin over chord and DAW baselines, and example-based input's advantage over text search [@kim2025amuse; @liang2024drawlody; @frid2020example]. And it licenses notation-surface integration, through Aria's 74,000 accepted suggestions, FlowComposer's eight-bar usage pattern, and professional composers asking for MusicXML interoperability and control preservation [@donahue2024hookpadaria; @papadopoulos2016flowcomposer; @huang2019bachdoodle; @newman2023perceptions]. What it does not license is any claim that the music will be better: Amuse found no difference in externally judged quality, Composer's Assistant 2 found parity with real music rather than superiority, and the CMU experiment leaves live the hypothesis that assistance can subtract [@kim2025amuse; @malandro2024composersassistant2; @oros2026cmu]. The studio's defensible claim is about agency, control and the shape of the work, and should be evaluated as such.

The gaps are as instructive as the findings. Sample sizes are small and skew novice: the median controlled study here has under twenty participants, and the professional studies are mostly interview-based [@newman2023perceptions; @deruty2022sonycsl; @cavez2024challenges]. There is one four-week field study of a music co-creation tool [@krol2025ownership] and one deployed system with public acceptance telemetry [@donahue2024hookpadaria]; nothing in between, and nothing longitudinal on a notation-native compiler. No study evaluates an *annotation* as the unit of analysis — no published work reports whether a composer's typed, drawn or hummed instruction was honoured, the studio's central measurable claim, and the instrument for it does not exist. Nor is there any established way to evaluate the quality of an AI's explanation of what it did, which COFI shows is unmeasured because it is unbuilt [@rezwana2023cofi].

Practically, the studio should instrument itself from the first release rather than plan a study later. Acceptance telemetry per annotation type, recompile counts and kept-span lengths cost little to log and answer questions no questionnaire reaches; the CSI-plus-agency battery and a similarity-matched paired listening protocol belong in standing infrastructure, run at every major interaction change; and one longitudinal cohort, on the Rhapsody Refiner model, should run continuously. Two of the gaps above are themselves candidate contributions: an *annotation-fidelity* measure — did the compiler honour this mark, and can the composer tell — and an evaluation of AI→human explanation quality on notation, for which RefinPaint's "where to modify" critic and ReaLJam's commitment display are the only prior art [@ramoneda2024refinpaint; @scarlatos2025realjam]. Either would give the studio empirical claims no existing system can make.
