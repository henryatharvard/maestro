## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 The paradigm problem

By September 2026 the public face of music AI is a text box. A user types a sentence, waits, and receives a finished stereo master with vocals. Suno's models advanced from v3 to v5.5 in roughly two years, Udio reached commercial scale before settling with two major labels, and Google's Lyria family moved from research demo to a consumer product line and a real-time API [@suno2026; @udio2025; @google2025lyria2]. Open weights followed the same shape: ACE-Step, YuE, DiffRhythm, SongGen and HeartMuLa all take a text prompt and lyrics and return a song [@gong2026acestep15; @yuan2025yue; @ning2025diffrhythm; @liu2025songgen; @heartmula2026].

For a composer this is the wrong shape. A prompt is a very low-bandwidth channel for musical intent, the output arrives as an opaque mixdown with no handle for correction, and the human contribution collapses to prompt authorship and curation. The evidence that professionals experience it this way is now direct. Seventeen producers given MusicGen and Demucs reported "creative misalignment" and pain aligning generated material to their own tempo, key and beat grids, and asked for control and editability above all [@ronchini2025ttmuserstudy]. Six professional composers interviewed for ISMIR 2023 asked for MusicXML and MIDI interoperability, open and explainable models, and preservation of control [@newman2023perceptions]. An eighteen-month study embedded with six professional acts at Sony CSL found that practitioners want to *pull* material rather than be *pushed* it, to prime models on their own audio, and to stay inside the tools they already use [@deruty2022sonycsl]. A four-week ecological study of a variation tool found that transformations of the user's own authored material preserved creative ownership while automatic completion was rejected outright [@krol2025ownership].

The market itself has been drifting toward the composer's shape without adopting its premise. Suno added Covers, Personas and Replace Section, then a DAW-like Studio in September 2025, then MIDI import and editing in August 2026; Udio added inpainting; Google's Music AI Sandbox added region editing and a multitrack view; Stable Audio 2.5 and ElevenLabs Music advertise inpainting; ACE-Step ships repainting, lyric editing and vocal-to-accompaniment [@suno2026; @udio2025; @google2025lyria2; @novack2025arc; @elevenlabs2025music; @gong2026acestep15]. Every one of these is an admission that one-shot generation is insufficient, retrofitted onto an architecture that was not designed for iteration.

This review surveys the space from the opposite starting point. It asks what the literature and the existing systems offer to a studio in which the composer authors the material, marks it up, and asks a machine to **compile** a fuller realization under those marks — then edits, re-annotates, and recompiles. The loop is

> compose → annotate → compile → edit → annotate → compile → …

with editing understood broadly: opening the notation and changing it by hand, scribbling on it, humming a line, attaching an example recording, a picture, a video or a sentence of prose. The word *compile* is deliberate. It implies a source that a human owns, intermediate representations that can be inspected, a deterministic and repeatable build, and a build log that explains what the machine did with each instruction.

### 1.2 Scope, method and corpus

The corpus is 464 annotated entries gathered in September 2026 across eight parallel research clusters, yielding 399 unique bibliographic records. Coverage runs from Hiller and Isaacson's *Illiac Suite* in 1957 to preprints of mid-2026, with emphasis on 2023–2026. The clusters were seeded by a practising composer-engineer's own reading list — Music Transformer and Anna Huang's lineage, the CMU cluster around Chris Donahue's Generative Creativity Lab, Amuse, ExpressEdit, music21 and MIT's computational music theory teaching, Suno and ACE-Step, and a January 2026 CMU result on human versus AI-assisted creativity — and then expanded systematically: symbolic generation and editing; audio generation and controllable audio; HCI and creativity-support research on co-creation; multimodal input, annotation and the music-as-code lineage; infrastructure, datasets, computational music theory and education; and the historical, commercial, evaluative and legal context.

Each entry was recorded with a verification level. Roughly two-thirds were verified against a primary source fetched during the survey — an arXiv abstract page, an ACM Digital Library record, a project page, a repository README, a court docket or an official release note. Entries marked *partial* were confirmed only from secondary pages or search snippets, and a small number marked *unverified* rest on recall alone. Claims drawn from *partial* and *unverified* entries are hedged in the prose that follows, and precise figures are omitted where the source could not be re-read. Three limitations follow from the method. Search budgets were exhausted in several clusters, so older canonical works (Koenig's *Project 1*, Vercoe's Synthetic Performer, GenJam) are recorded at lower confidence than 2025–2026 preprints. Non-English literature, the commercial Japanese and Chinese ecosystems beyond the systems named here, and the substantial NIME and ICMC hardware-instrument literature are under-represented. And the fast-moving product and legal material is a snapshot: it is accurate as of early September 2026 and will decay quickly.

### 1.3 The organizing taxonomy

Existing surveys of music AI are organized by technique, domain or task [@ji2023survey; @le2025nlpsurvey; @ma2024foundation; @kader2025survey]. Those axes are the right ones for a modeller and the wrong ones for a builder, because they do not say where a given contribution sits in a composer's working day. The companion taxonomy therefore uses the loop itself as its trunk — **Compose (S1)**, **Annotate (S2)**, **Compile (S3)**, **Edit (S4)**, with **Play (S5)** as the real-time branch — surrounded by **Foundations (F)** that precede it, a **Substrate (B)** of representations, engines, toolkits and data beneath it, **Context (C)** of products, law and economics around it, and **Evidence and Evaluation (E)** as the meta-layer that decides whether any of it works. A second, orthogonal view codes each work on ten dimensions: output domain, task framing, control granularity, input modalities, initiative and timing, communication direction, editability of the intermediate representation, integration surface, evidence level, and openness and provenance.

Three pieces of vocabulary are borrowed rather than invented. Rowe's 1993 distinctions — score-driven versus performance-driven input, transformative versus generative versus sequenced response, and the instrument versus player paradigm — describe the studio precisely as a score-driven, transformative system in the instrument paradigm [@rowe1993interactive]. Kantosalo and Toivonen's alternating versus task-divided co-creativity names the turn structure [@kantosalo2016modes]. Rezwana and Maher's COFI supplies the two communication directions and, in coding 92 co-creative systems, the finding that the AI-to-human direction is almost entirely undesigned [@rezwana2023cofi].

Sections 2 through 12 follow the taxonomy's order. Section 13 consolidates the gaps into a research agenda.

### 1.4 Five theses this review establishes

**First, infilling under hard constraints — not generation from scratch — is the primitive that implements the loop.** Coconet framed this in 2017 in almost the founder's words, observing that composers write "in a nonlinear fashion, scribbling motifs here and there, often revisiting choices previously made," and modelling it as blocked-Gibbs completion of a partially masked score [@huang2017coconet]. Nine years of work has made it mature: bar-by-track masking in MMM and MIDI-GPT, arbitrary track-measure selection with roughly ten fine-grained controls in Composer's Assistant, anticipation tokens that let any subset of notes be pinned as hard constraints in the Anticipatory Music Transformer, and arbitrary piano-roll masks in Polyffusion and GETMusic [@ens2020mmm; @pasquier2025midigpt; @malandro2024composersassistant2; @thickstun2024anticipatory; @min2023polyffusion; @lv2023getmusic]. The strongest deployment evidence in the entire corpus is Hookpad Aria's: about 74,000 of 318,000 span-level suggestions accepted by roughly 3,000 users inside a lead-sheet editor — about a 23% acceptance rate, reported in full under E3 [@donahue2024hookpadaria].

**Second, interface steerability moves the outcomes that matter to composers at least as much as model capability does.** The only two studies that varied model and interface independently found that steering tools produced the larger gains in control, ownership and self-efficacy, while the more expressive model produced better listener-judged musicality — the two are complementary, not substitutes [@louie2020cococo; @louie2022expressive]. The corollary from Cococo is a design rule: generative power should be "partitioned into smaller, semantically meaningful tools" rather than exposed as one button. MMM-C's single-slider design confirms the negative case, scoring acceptable usability while its users' desire for more control averaged about 9.5 out of 10 [@tchemeube2023mmmc].

**Third, every input modality that works routes through an editable symbolic representation.** Humming becomes MIDI or a lead sheet, drawing becomes notes, an image becomes chords, and in each case the symbolic layer is what gives the human a correction handle [@bittner2022basicpitch; @donahue2022sheetsage; @liang2024drawlody; @kim2025amuse]. Systems that map a loose gesture straight to audio are impressive and uncorrectable [@donahue2023singsong; @tian2025vidmuse]. Amuse is the cleanest demonstration of the alternative: a multimodal model proposes keywords the user can edit, and those keywords drive chord candidates filtered by rejection sampling against a chord prior trained on real music, with significant gains in inspiration support and controllability over baselines [@kim2025amuse].

**Fourth, the annotation layer is the studio's genuine white space.** The pieces exist separately. Score annotation already has standardised, machine-readable, musical-time-anchored formats in MEI, Dezrann's labels, Humdrum and RomanText [@pugin2014verovio; @giraud2018dezrann; @huron1995humdrum; @gotham2019wheninrome]. Analysis models can propose harmonic and structural labels well enough to be corrected rather than trusted, with full-label Roman-numeral accuracy of about 51% for AugmentedNet and 62% for RNBert, and key accuracy of 82–83% [@napoleslopez2021augmentednet; @sailor2024rnbert]. Generation systems have converged on a control vocabulary — instrument, note density, polyphony, pitch range, chord per beat, section label, style — that reads like a list of things a composer would write on a score [@vonrutte2023figaro; @guo2022musiac; @chen2024sympac]. And the interaction pattern for turning free-form marks into machine instructions has been demonstrated in video, where ExpressEdit parses natural language plus frame sketches into temporal, spatial, operation and parameter references, resolves each with a per-modality resolver, and returns inspectable, editable suggestions [@tilekbay2024expressedit]. No system found in this survey combines them for music. Treating a composer's margin notes as a compilable instruction set is the most defensible research contribution available here.

**Fifth, a symbolic-first, human-authored studio sits on materially better legal and ethical ground than the prompt-to-audio alternative.** The Munich Regional Court held in November 2025 that memorisation of lyrics in model weights constitutes reproduction, and extended the reasoning to Suno in a judgment of 31 July 2026 in which the text-and-data-mining exception was found inapplicable [@lgmunich2025gemaopenai; @lgmunich2026gemasuno]. In the United States, the Copyright Office's second report and the *Thaler* line hold that human authorship is required for protection, with certiorari denied in March 2026 — which means human-authored, human-edited notation is protectable where prompt-only output largely is not [@usco2025part2; @thaler2025perlmutter]. Meanwhile licence-clean training material for symbolic models genuinely exists: PDMX offers more than 250,000 public-domain MusicXML scores under CC-BY, and OpenScore's critical editions are CC0 [@long2024pdmx; @gotham2022openscore]. Creator surveys point the same way, with a majority accepting AI that assists the creative process while overwhelming majorities demand consent and disclosure [@apraamcos2024ai].
