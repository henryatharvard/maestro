## 8. Edit: revising and iterating (S4)

Editing is the stage at which the studio's thesis is either honoured or lost. If the composer can only accept or reject what the machine produced, the loop degenerates into curation whatever the interface promises; if the composer can select a region, say or draw or sing what should change, inspect the machine's proposal, alter it, and keep a record of what happened, then generation is genuinely subordinate to authorship. This section surveys the general HCI paradigms for machine-mediated editing (S4.1), symbolic transformation, variation and proofreading of music the composer already wrote (S4.2), audio editing and inpainting (S4.3), the neglected reverse channel in which the system explains or previews what it intends (S4.4), and the history, alternatives and branching machinery that makes iteration recoverable (S4.5). Two asymmetries structure it: editing is far better developed in audio than in symbolic music — eleven primary works in S4.3 against two in S4.2, though the symbolic surface is where edits are semantically meaningful — and the human→AI direction far better developed than the AI→human direction, which has two primary entries in total, a finding independently established by @rezwana2023cofi.

### 8.1 S4.1 Editing paradigms from HCI at large

The normative baseline is @shneiderman1983direct, whose three properties of direct manipulation — continuous visual representation of the object of interest, physical actions in place of complex syntax, and rapid, incremental, reversible operations whose effect is immediately visible — were formulated explicitly against command languages. Prompt-based music generation is a command language with a probabilistic interpreter: the object of interest is not continuously represented (it exists only as the last rendered output), the actions are syntax rather than gestures, and operations are neither incremental nor reversible. Judged by those criteria the dominant paradigm of 2023–2026 music AI is a regression, and the question is what it takes to recover direct manipulation without losing the expressive reach of language.

DirectGPT is the most precise answer available. @masson2024directgpt built a direct-manipulation layer over a large language model in which generated objects remain continuously represented and selectable, toolbar operators reuse prompt syntax, outputs can be dragged and selected to compose the next prompt, and every operation is undoable; the system translates manipulation into an engineered prompt rather than asking the user to write one. Across text, code and vector-graphics editing tasks, participants were reported 50% faster, issuing 50% fewer prompts and prompts 72% shorter than with a conversational baseline. The mechanism behind the shortening is the load-bearing insight: when the selection carries the scope and the referent, the utterance need only carry the intent — "make bars 17–24 of the second violin part sparser" collapses to a selection plus "sparser."

Instruction-conditioned editing supplies the model-side counterpart. @brooks2023instructpix2pix trained a conditional diffusion model mapping an (image, edit instruction) pair to an edited image in one forward pass, and solved the absent-dataset problem by *synthesising* training triples — GPT-3 generating instructions and edited captions, Stable Diffusion with prompt-to-prompt generating the before/after image pairs — so no human editing labels were required, establishing instruction editing as a trainable task rather than an inference-time trick. Its transfer to music is a data-generation recipe as much as an architecture: the missing corpus for annotation-driven symbolic editing is a set of (score, annotation, edited score) triples, bootstrappable by applying known transformations in reverse and having a language model describe them.

ExpressEdit shows what the interface around such a model should look like. Its pipeline and study figures are given under S2.4; what matters here is the contract it establishes. @tilekbay2024expressedit let users describe an edit in language *and* sketch on the artifact, parsed the request into typed references, resolved each with a modality-appropriate resolver, and returned suggestions the user could accept, reject or adjust, with a panel exposing how each reference had been interpreted. The distribution of outcomes is the lesson: fewer than half of suggestions were accepted as offered, and a clear majority of the edits that survived into the final result were *modified* suggestions. Most accepted work was therefore neither the machine's proposal nor the human's unaided edit but the human's revision of the proposal — which means the unit of delivery must be an editable draft, not a committed change.

Three older lineages fill out the paradigm. @igarashi1999teddy established the sketch-interface contract in 3D modelling: accept an ambiguous gesture, infer a plausible complete object, display it immediately, and let further gestures refine it, with no dialog boxes in the loop. @cypher1993wwid collected the programming-by-demonstration systems built on the premise that knowing how to perform a task should suffice to create a program performing it, the user's role being to correct over- and under-generalisation of the demonstrated example; read musically, an attached reference recording is a demonstration and "voice the chords like this" a generalisation request whose intended aspect the composer must be able to name. The live- and projectional-programming line contributes three further properties: Tanimoto's liveness levels, describing how immediately edits are reflected in running output; Hazel's typed holes, which give a dynamic semantics for *incomplete* programs so that evaluation proceeds around the holes and every editor state yields feedback; and projectional editors such as JetBrains MPS, which edit an abstract syntax tree directly and project it into several synchronised notations at once [@omar2019hazel]. A score with explicit "to be composed" regions that still compiles and plays, re-renders on every annotation, and is editable as notation, piano roll, chord chart or text is the musical reading of those properties, and what makes "compile" more than a metaphor (see S3.6 and @kim2026decomposer).

The transferable pattern across these lineages is one contract: *select a region of the artefact, express intent in whatever modality is handiest, and receive inspectable, editable suggestions rather than a committed change.* Each clause carries weight independently — selection makes utterances short and referents unambiguous, modality freedom lets a composer hum a counter-line rather than describe it, and suggestion-rather-than-commitment preserves the reversibility Shneiderman required while matching ExpressEdit's evidence that revision is the normal case. Conversational music systems violate all three clauses, and their evaluations register it: Loop Copilot's eight users rated it usable (SUS 75.3 ± 15.3) and valued it for inspiration but complained of limited musical-attribute control and poor prompt responsiveness, and asked for DAW integration [@zhang2023loopcopilot].

### 8.2 S4.2 Symbolic editing, variation and proofreading

The strongest single piece of evidence for the studio's stance comes from a study of a deliberately modest tool. @krol2025ownership built Rhapsody Refiner, which takes a musician's own MIDI and produces *variations* by selectively masking notes and re-predicting them with MusicBERT under user-set parameters; it cannot compose from scratch, and that dependence on strong human input was a design commitment, not a limitation. It was evaluated in a four-week ecological study with eight practising musicians — songwriters, producers, educators, instrumentalists — combining tutorials, independent in-the-wild use, journals, logs, interviews and thematic analysis. The findings' asymmetry is the point. Participants valued the tool for generating "moments, not whole ideas"; because output depended on material they had authored, they reported ownership of both artefact and *process*; and they explicitly rejected automatic completion, declining to have the system finish their work even when it could. Its imperfections and randomness were reported as productive, prompting creative problem-solving rather than read as errors. The generalisation is narrow but exactly the one the studio needs: transforming authored material preserves creative ownership, whereas supplying unauthored material displaces it — a distinction invisible to any evaluation measuring only output quality, and consistent with @suh2021socialglue's warning that a generous generative model can shift collaborators from co-composing to co-*producing*.

Attribute-conditioned rewriting is the best-developed technical form of "transform what I wrote." MuseMorphose encodes each bar of an existing pop-piano piece to a latent with a Transformer VAE and decodes it under user-set *bar-level* attributes — rhythmic intensity and polyphony, eight levels each — rewriting a song's texture bar by bar while preserving its content; @wu2023musemorphose reported gains over MusicVAE-style RNN baselines in fidelity, attribute-control accuracy and diversity across full songs. Music FaderNets learned disentangled "faders" for rhythm and note density in a VAE whose Gaussian-mixture latent captured *arousal* semi-supervised from only 1% labelled data, grounding a high-level intention ("more intense here") in inspectable low-level features [@tan2020fadernets]. FIGARO and MuseCoco apply the same manoeuvre at compile rather than edit time (see S3.3) [@vonrutte2023figaro; @lu2023musecoco], and the Latent Fourier Transform generalises it to timescale, masking latent frequency bands as an "equalizer for musical patterns" that keeps phrase-level shape while varying surface detail, on qualitative evidence [@wang2026latentft].

Natural-language editing of symbolic music has exactly one careful study. @zhang2025groove asked whether general-purpose LLMs can edit an existing drum groove, with no training, from instructions of three kinds — specific ("add a cymbal on beat 1"), descriptive ("reduce hi-hat activity") and stylistic ("make it funkier") — representing the groove in a compact textual "drumroll" notation of one line per instrument and one character per sixteenth. Its methodological contribution is the evaluation: in place of ground-truth edited grooves, which do not exist and would be non-unique anyway, it used expert-authored *unit tests*, which agreed with professional musicians at 89% true-positive and 94% true-negative rates; the best of eight models (gpt-4.1-mini) passed 68%, and reasoning-tuned models helped. Two lessons follow: zero-shot instruction editing is feasible in a narrow, densely quantised domain and should not be assumed to transfer to voiced polyphony, and verification of an edit is a test-satisfaction problem, which fits the compiler framing exactly — an edit request is a specification, and specifications can be checked.

Three further works reach the same primitive from the compile side and are treated at length under S3.2, each contributing one lesson to the editing side. RefinPaint separates *where* to change from *how*, pairing a critic that identifies the weakest notes in context with an inpainting model that resamples them, so proofreading becomes iterative rather than wholesale [@ramoneda2024refinpaint]. Composer's Assistant 2 marks the fine-grained-control endpoint, letting the composer mark partial track-measures and constrain the refill along roughly ten named musical axes [@malandro2024composersassistant2]. FlowComposer supplies the interaction datum that matters most here: shipped in 2016 with region-level regeneration and conformance sliders, it was observed to serve *fragments*, composers rarely generating more than eight bars at a time [@papadopoulos2016flowcomposer]. Cococo's steering tools produced the same behaviour with measured effects on ownership and control [@louie2020cococo].

The node is nonetheless thin. Only two works surveyed here have symbolic editing as their *primary* contribution — MuseMorphose and "Not that Groove" — and one covers drum grooves only; everything else is an infilling system repurposed for editing, an attribute-conditioned generator, or a study of a research prototype. There is no symbolic analogue of InstructPix2Pix: no model trained on (score, instruction, edited score) triples, no public corpus of such triples, no benchmark for polyphonic edit fidelity. Given that a composer's edits are the operations for which the symbolic surface exists, this is the largest gap on the *editing* side.

### 8.3 S4.3 Audio editing and inpainting

Audio went the other way, and quickly. The zero-shot branch manipulates a pretrained generator's conditioning or noise trajectory. MusicMagus edits *generated* music by translating the text-embedding difference between a source and target word — "piano" to "guitar" — into a latent-space move, with cross-attention constraints preserving unedited attributes; it beat zero-shot and some supervised baselines on style and timbre transfer but was limited on real recordings [@zhang2024musicmagus]. That limitation motivated inversion: @manor2024zeta applied edit-friendly DDPM inversion to AudioLDM2, giving ZETA for text-based editing of *real* recordings with structure preserved and ZEUS for *unsupervised* discovery of semantic edit directions by PCA over posterior-mean directions, which yielded musically meaningful axes — adding or removing an instrument's participation, melodic improvisation — with no text at all. MelodyFlow adapted ReNoise latent inversion to flow matching with regularisation for fast zero-shot editing of existing stereo clips that changes genre and instrumentation while preserving melody and structure, outperforming ReNoise and DDIM inversion on editing consistency [@lelan2024melodyflow]. Text2FX is the outlier: rather than regenerate audio, it optimises *differentiable audio-effect* parameters (EQ, reverb) so the processed signal's CLAP embedding matches a prompt such as "warmer," leaving interpretable, further-editable parameters behind [@chu2025text2fx].

The trained branch is stronger. AUDIT was the first instruction-following audio editor, trained on (instruction, input audio, output audio) triplets to add, drop, replace, inpaint and super-resolve, establishing the natural-language-edit-over-a-clip pattern for general audio [@wang2023audit]. InstructME specialised it to music — add, remove, extract, replace and remix instruments, multi-round — and, importantly for a composer-centred tool, injected a chord-progression matrix as a condition so edits stay harmonically faithful [@han2023instructme]. Instruct-MusicGen reached comparable capability far more cheaply, adding audio- and text-fusion modules to a frozen MusicGen at roughly 8% extra parameters and 5,000 steps to follow "add, remove or separate a stem," matching task-specific models on Slakh- and MoisesDB-derived benchmarks with open code [@zhang2025instructmusicgen]. SongEditor extended editing to full songs *with vocals*, supporting segment-wise editing (regenerate a section with modified lyrics) and track-wise editing (vocals versus accompaniment) [@yang2025songeditor]. MuseControlLite is the most practical open recipe: 85M-parameter decoupled cross-attention adapters with rotary positional embeddings on Stable Audio Open, giving time-varying melody, rhythm and dynamics control *plus* inpainting and outpainting at 6.75× fewer trainable parameters than a ControlNet baseline, melody accuracy rising from 56.6% to 61.1% with RoPE [@tsai2025musecontrollite]. Adjacent designs make the mask structural rather than temporal: MusicGen-Stem trains one codec per stem so one stem can be regenerated against fixed others [@rouard2025musicgenstem], MAGNeT's re-mask-and-redecode is inherently local [@ziv2024magnet], and melody-conditioned ControlNet branches on Stable Audio's diffusion Transformer reportedly support "keep my melody, re-render everything else" from top-k constant-Q peaks [@hou2024melodycontrolnet].

Industry converged on the same primitive. @adobe2024musicgenaicontrol framed the problem publicly as generate-then-edit-with-musical-parameters (tempo, structure, intensity, section remix, reference melody) — technically a productisation of Music ControlNet and DITTO, never shipped [@wu2024musiccontrolnet; @novack2024ditto]. Stable Audio 2.5 exposes inpainting as an API — upload audio, pick a region, generate the fill — with three-minute generation in under two seconds via adversarial relativistic-contrastive post-training, whose open sibling renders twelve seconds of 44.1 kHz stereo in roughly 75 ms on an H100 [@novack2025arc]. The full-song platforms accreted region editing under user pressure: Suno's Replace Section, Udio's region inpainting, ElevenLabs's verse/chorus regeneration and ACE-Step's repainting are one operation under four names (see S3.9) [@suno2026; @udio2025; @elevenlabs2025music; @gong2025acestep].

Two conclusions follow. Region editing is table stakes in audio: every serious system of 2025–2026, open or closed, supports selecting a span and regenerating it, and sub-second inference makes doing so interactive. And natively mask-trained models beat post-hoc tricks. Inversion editing is elegant and needs no paired data, but trades quality against fidelity to unedited material, works best on model-generated audio, and offers no control surface beyond the prompt; models trained with masks or instruction triplets (AUDIT, InstructME, Instruct-MusicGen, SongEditor, MuseControlLite) expose an explicit editing interface, preserve context by construction, and — in InstructME's case — hold the composer's harmony fixed while doing it. The studio's audio stage should therefore rest on a mask-trained, control-adapted open renderer, not on inversion of a text-to-music model.

### 8.4 S4.4 AI→human feedback, explanation and plan preview

@rezwana2023cofi coded 92 existing co-creative systems against the COFI framework and found that the field concentrates on AI generative ability and *almost entirely lacks AI→human communication* — explanations, statements of intent, expressions of confidence — which they argue is precisely what distinguishes a partner from a tool. Their companion study compared two versions of a co-creative sketching system, one contributing only sketches and one also communicating its intentions and reactions; the communicating version was perceived as more collaborative, engaging, partner-like and reliable, though some found it intrusive [@rezwana2022perceptions]. This taxonomy's tally corroborates the diagnosis: two primary entries here against eleven for audio editing.

What little exists points in consistent directions. RefinPaint's feedback model answers *where* to modify before the inpainter answers *how* — an AI-produced annotation on the composer's own score, and the only music system surveyed that marks locations rather than replacing content [@ramoneda2024refinpaint]. Cococo's users, given voice lanes, semantic sliders, an example-similarity slider and multiple alternatives, spontaneously developed "debugging" strategies to work out what the model was doing; against a generate-and-accept baseline in a within-subjects study with 21 novices, comprehensibility rose to 5.3 from 3.2 and controllability to 5.9 from 3.5 [@louie2020cococo]. That users invent debugging behaviour in the absence of explanation is an argument for supplying it. The XAIxArts line makes controls legible from the model side by regularising MeasureVAE's first four latent dimensions onto rhythmic complexity, note range, note density and average interval jump, with visualisations of each [@bryankinns2021xai]; @scurto2021coexplorer inverted the channel, letting like/dislike feedback steer an agent's parameter-space exploration.

The plan-preview pattern is the most directly transferable. ReaLJam displayed the accompaniment agent's *upcoming* chords in a waterfall view split into a committed window that cannot change and an adaptive window that may; five of six experienced pianists wanted the visible preview, and tunable protocol parameters absorbed the wide variation in their other preferences [@scarlatos2025realjam]. ExpressEdit's Examine panel is the non-real-time equivalent, showing how each temporal, spatial, operational and parameter reference was resolved, and was rated 6.1 of 7 [@tilekbay2024expressedit]. Side Views generalises both as persistent, on-demand previews of what an operation *would* do before committing [@terry2002sideviews]. Constraint validators occupy the same slot from the checking side, as in MultiVerse's rule-based enforcement of creator-authored lyric constraints [@wang2026multiverse], as does test-based verification of edits [@zhang2025groove].

The compiler framing has an unusually natural answer here, which is reason to keep the metaphor. A compiler does not silently emit a binary: it emits diagnostics naming a location, a rule and a reason, and a build log recording what it did with which inputs. The musical analogue is a per-compile log stating which annotation governed which bars, which model or solver handled each span, which alternatives were considered, where the system was least confident, and which constraints it could not satisfy — every line anchored to a score element identifier and therefore clickable. This subsumes RefinPaint's where-to-modify as a diagnostic, ReaLJam's committed/tentative split as a preview, ExpressEdit's Examine as reference resolution, and Cococo's debugging as an intended workflow rather than a coping strategy. Nothing in the surveyed literature implements it for music.

### 8.5 S4.5 History, versioning, alternatives and branching

The HCI foundation is the alternatives-and-subjunctive lineage collected by @terry2002sideviews Side Views' persistent on-demand previews of possible outcomes, Juxtapose's parallel authoring of interface variants with linked editing and runtime tuning, and Lunzer and Hornbæk's subjunctive interfaces for viewing and controlling alternative scenarios side by side — all resting on the claim that near-term experimentation, variation and comparison are core creative needs that premature commitment suppresses, with lab studies reporting more alternatives explored under parallelism. @grossman2010chronicle attacked provenance instead: Chronicle records a graphical document's full video and event history so a user can click any region of the *final* artefact, see the workflow, tools and settings that produced it, and play the sequence back; its study found this useful and easy to use. Luminate updated alternatives for LLMs by having the model first generate a design space's *dimensions* and then populate a structured grid users browse, filter and combine; fourteen professional writers found it useful for exploration [@suh2024luminate].

Music-specific machinery is sparse but real. @foscarin2019diff defined a diff procedure for *scores* at the graphical-notation level, converting each bar and voice to a tree, combining sequence edit distance over bars with tree edit distance over beaming and tuplet hierarchies, and rendering two Verovio scores side by side with differences highlighted; validated against OMR output, it was motivated by collaborative editing and version control. It is the only research artefact for musical diff found in this review, and without one there is no way to show a composer what a compile changed. On the product side, Flat.io auto-syncs every edit, creates a major version at most every fifty modifications, lets free users restore the ten most recent major versions and paid users scrub *every individual change* with a video-like slider, marks change locations on a score scrollbar with per-contributor colour coding, and exports any historical state to MusicXML or MIDI [@flat2026history]. Fine-grained, attributable linear notation history is therefore a solved delivery problem; the DAW-project analogues, Splice Studio's git-like commit history and Blend's project forking, are both reportedly defunct, suggesting limited pull for file-level versioning of opaque projects. mei-friend takes a third route, pairing an MEI code view with live Verovio rendering, committing encodings to GitHub, and attaching standards-based Web Annotations to score elements by identifier — at once a projectional-editing pattern, a git history for symbolic music and an annotation anchor, on thin published documentation [@goebl2023meifriend].

What does not exist anywhere is branch and merge with *musical* semantics. Score diffs exist, per-edit linear histories exist in products, alternatives interfaces exist in generic creative tools, and generation systems produce candidate sets — Cococo's alternatives, Calliope's batch generation of up to a thousand candidates for selected bars [@tchemeube2022calliope], Magenta Studio's Interpolate [@roberts2019magentastudio] — but no system lets a composer fork a passage into two arrangements, develop both, and merge the horn writing of one into the string writing of the other with conflicts reported musically. Luminate's dimension-first grid and Chronicle's click-to-provenance likewise have no music analogue.

Provenance logging is also where the editing loop meets the authorship argument, which raises the stakes above convenience. @micchi2021ikeepcounting documented more than twenty-five distinct human interventions in one AI-assisted song — structure from a Markov model, chords from an LSTM, lyrics from GPT-2, human filtering of candidates, human-composed verse melodies, human arrangement and production — and argued that deliberate selection and modification of machine material *constitutes* authorship, releasing the result under CC BY-SA. The U.S. Copyright Office's 2025 copyrightability report reached a compatible legal position: prompts alone do not confer authorship, but human-authored expressive inputs, creative selection and arrangement, and modifications of AI output are protectable to the extent of the human contribution, case by case [@usco2025part2]. A studio that logs every annotation, compile, accepted or rejected suggestion and manual edit against score element identifiers is therefore not merely building an undo stack but generating the evidentiary record on which the composer's claim rests. Chronicle's click-to-history, Flat's contributor colouring, mei-friend's commits and Foscarin et al.'s diff are its four components, and no system combines them.

### 8.6 Implications for the studio

The clearest design conclusion is that the edit stage should be a *selection-scoped suggestion pipeline*, not a chat window and not a regenerate button. The contract recurring across DirectGPT, ExpressEdit, Teddy, programming by demonstration and every successful music system in S4.2 is identical: the composer selects a region of the score, expresses intent in whatever modality is nearest to hand — typed instruction, sketch on the staff, hummed line, reference clip, slider — and receives inspectable, editable proposals with their interpretation exposed, which the composer modifies before accepting. Proposals must therefore land in the editable notation surface, carry the reference resolution that produced them, and be revisable without a second round-trip through the model. FlowComposer's decade-old observation that composers rarely regenerate more than eight bars, and Rhapsody Refiner's participants valuing "moments, not whole ideas," together fix the default edit unit at a phrase — not a section and never a song.

The technical gap the studio is best placed to close is symbolic instruction editing. Audio has mask-trained editors, instruction-tuned editors, inversion editors, region-editing APIs and shipped product features; symbolic music has attribute-conditioned rewriting on piano, infilling repurposed as editing, and one careful study of zero-shot natural-language editing confined to drum grooves. The route through is visible even though no one has taken it: build a (score, annotation, edited score) corpus by InstructPix2Pix's synthetic-triple recipe, inverting known musical transformations and having a language model describe them; adopt ExpressEdit's four-part reference grammar as the annotation schema, with temporal references resolving to bars, beats and formal sections, spatial references to staves, voices and registers, operations to a closed vocabulary of musical edits and parameters to sliders and reference examples alike; adopt "Not that Groove"'s unit-test methodology as the verification layer, so an edit request becomes a checkable specification; and keep the compile step deterministic where a program-based intermediate representation allows. Because the studio is symbolic-first, its edits are semantically inspectable in a way audio edits never are, which is what makes testing and diffing possible at all.

The half of the loop no existing system builds is the return channel, and it is the half a compiler makes obvious. Every component of the build log sketched in S4.4 exists somewhere — RefinPaint's where-to-modify, ReaLJam's committed-versus-tentative preview, Side Views' on-demand previews, Chronicle's click-to-provenance, Foscarin et al.'s score diff so that reviewing an AI edit resembles reviewing a patch — and none has been assembled for music; above them sits the branching layer nobody has built, forked arrangements of one annotated passage compared along explicit musical dimensions as in Luminate and merged with conflicts reported musically. That log is also the authorship record. Between @micchi2021ikeepcounting's argument from documented intervention and the Copyright Office's position that human selection, arrangement and modification are protectable to the extent of the human contribution [@usco2025part2], a studio keeping a complete, inspectable trail of who changed what, when and under which annotation produces not merely a better editing experience but the proof that the composer wrote the piece.
