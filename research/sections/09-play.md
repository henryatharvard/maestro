## 9. Play: real-time co-performance (S5)

The compose–annotate–compile–edit loop is turn-taking: the composer acts, the compiler responds, the composer edits. Play is the branch of the same loop in which the response must arrive within a beat. It matters to this studio for two reasons. The founder is a keyboardist, and an arrangement that can be *played with* on a Nord Stage over MIDI is a different test of a compiled score than one that can only be auditioned. And the real-time literature is the oldest continuous line of "machines that listen and respond" in music; it has already worked out vocabulary (Rowe's score-driven versus performance-driven input, instrument versus player paradigm), interaction patterns (anticipation displays, kill-switches, per-user protocol settings) and training recipes (RL against coherence rewards with an offline teacher) that the turn-taking studio can reuse [@rowe1993interactive; @scarlatos2025realjam; @wu2024realchords]. In the taxonomy's terms Play is D5 *simultaneous* rather than human-initiated, and it forces latency and future-visibility trade-offs that offline compilation can ignore. This section moves from score following (S5.1) through improvisation partners (S5.2) and RL-tuned or streaming neural models (S5.3) to the design spaces, pedagogy and games that frame the practice (S5.4).

### 9.1 S5.1 Score following and accompaniment

The score-driven pole of Rowe's taxonomy was established in a single year by two systems. Dannenberg's on-line accompaniment algorithm (ICMC 1984) matched a live monophonic performance against a stored score by dynamic programming and drove accompaniment playback from the resulting tempo estimate, demonstrated live with trumpet [@dannenberg1984online]. Vercoe's Synthetic Performer, demonstrated at IRCAM the same year, reportedly tracked a flautist through pitch and fingering sensors and learned the player's tempo tendencies across rehearsals; it heads an MIT line that runs through Csound's orchestra-and-score compile model to the present [@vercoe1984syntheticperformer]. Dannenberg's Human-Computer Music Performance programme (2011–2014) generalised from soloist-plus-accompanist to the computer as a *performing partner* in popular-music ensembles: beat- and section-level synchronisation, human cueing roles and "active scores," machine-readable, structure-aware scores that can be re-sequenced live with repeats and cues, with the Accomplice keyboard-accompaniment project among its offshoots [@dannenberg2014hcmp]. Active scores are an early representation designed for both humans and machines to edit and follow, and thus a direct ancestor of an annotated, compilable score. At MIT, Egozy's group has reported real-time in-browser score following by online time warping (Caren and Egozy, JAES 2025), and ConcertCue streams time-synchronised programme notes to audience phones cued to musical events, deployed with the Boston Symphony Orchestra and other ensembles [@egozy2016harmonix; @egozy2018concertcue]. ConcertCue addresses listeners rather than generators, but its data structure, typed content anchored to score positions and delivered at performance time, is the one the studio's annotation layer needs (S2.1). The evidence level throughout S5.1 is engineering demonstration and deployment rather than controlled study, and by the taxonomy's own count it is the thinnest node in Play.

### 9.2 S5.2 Improvisation partners

The performance-driven, player-paradigm pole has an equally long history. George Lewis's Voyager, begun in 1987, listens through pitch-to-MIDI and generates multiple independent voices that may respond to, ignore or initiate against the human improviser; Lewis frames it explicitly as *not* an instrument the human controls but a partner rooted in Afrological improvisation aesthetics, the far pole of the control axis [@lewis2000toomanynotes]. Biles's GenJam (ICMC 1994) evolved populations of jazz licks over a chord progression with a human "mentor" supplying real-time good/bad judgments as the fitness function, later trading fours with the player; it is remembered for exposing the *fitness bottleneck* of human-in-the-loop evaluation and is the earliest formalisation of annotation as fitness, the primitive form of the studio's annotate-then-regenerate loop [@biles1994genjam]. Pachet's Continuator learned a variable-order Markov model of a player's MIDI phrases in real time and continued each phrase in the same style when the player stopped, with reported studies by Addessi and Pachet in which this reflexive interaction increased children's engagement; Flow Machines generalised the idea to *Markov constraints*, style plus hard constraints on meter, harmony and position, and to lead-sheet generation in FlowComposer (S3.2) [@pachet2003continuator; @papadopoulos2016flowcomposer]. IRCAM's OMax and Somax2 practise "stylistic reinjection," learning a Factor Oracle over the live stream and improvising by recombining the performer's *own* material, with Somax2 adding reactive listening to the current harmonic context; the approach needs no external corpus, an ethically clean route to style [@assayag2006omax].

Two recent systems show what neural models add. Notochord (AIMC 2022) is an autoregressive model over MIDI *sub-events* (instrument, pitch, time, velocity) trained on Lakh MIDI with under 10 ms latency; because each sub-event is sampled separately, a performer can fix or constrain any of them, yielding harmonisers, improvisers and likelihood-based interfaces from one MIT-licensed model, the clearest real-time analogue of editing a note in the score and letting the model re-condition [@shepardson2022notochord]. The jam_bot (ISMIR 2025) is a stage system built with Dream Theater keyboardist Jordan Rudess: a Music Transformer fine-tuned on Rudess's own recorded basslines, chords and melodies, prompted into distinct roles (lead, accompaniment, call-and-response), driven by a low-latency scheduler, with a preview screen of upcoming decisions, restart and kill-switch controls to preserve performer agency, and a kinetic sculpture externalising the model's state; it premiered in September 2024 after a year of weekly co-design and was evaluated by performance rather than controlled study [@blanchard2025jambot; @huang2018musictransformer]. It demonstrates personalised symbolic models fine-tuned on one artist's material with explicit role assignment, and repeats the pattern of previewing AI intent before it commits [@huang2025creativityinteraction].

### 9.3 S5.3 RL-tuned and live neural models

Two earlier neural systems set the terms. Piano Genie (IUI 2019) decoded eight buttons in real time into plausible 88-key piano music through an RNN autoencoder with a discrete bottleneck constrained so that button contour maps to pitch contour; the human supplies rhythm, phrasing and rough contour and the model compiles exact pitches, the real-time cousin of humming or scribbling a line [@donahue2019pianogenie]. Co-Explorer (TOCHI 2021) used deep reinforcement learning to explore a synthesizer's parameter space while adapting to the sound designer's like/dislike feedback, evaluated through observational studies and a workshop with professional sound designers, a concrete design for feedback-as-steering [@scurto2021coexplorer].

The ReaLchords line is the most complete recent programme. ReaLchords (ICML 2024) is an *online* chord-accompaniment model for a live melody: frame-based at sixteenth-note resolution with a 5,041-chord vocabulary, pretrained by maximum likelihood on about 38k Hooktheory melody–chord pairs and RL-fine-tuned with self-supervised reward models scoring harmonic and temporal coherence plus a KL term distilling from an offline teacher that sees the future melody, "forcing anticipation" [@wu2024realchords]. Note-in-chord ratio rose from 37.0% for the online MLE model to 54.3%; the RL model recovered from a tritone transposition at beat 17 within two to three beats where MLE never did; and ten musicians in 192 pairwise comparisons significantly preferred it [@wu2024realchords]. ReaLJam (CHI EA 2025) wrapped it in a web interface in which the agent continually predicts how the performance will unfold and shows upcoming chords in a *waterfall* descending toward the keyboard, committed chords opaque and revisable predictions translucent, with round-trip latency under 100 ms at 150 BPM [@scarlatos2025realjam]. In a study with six experienced musicians over 60-minute sessions, enjoyment averaged 4.3/5 but perceived adaptation only 2.7/5; RL-trained models beat the pretrained one on every measure; five of six wanted visible chord previews; participants valued surprise that still matched expectation and wanted genre control and structure awareness (agents ignored four-bar phrase structure); and preferences for protocol settings varied so strongly by individual that per-user customisation was concluded to be essential [@scarlatos2025realjam]. GAPT (ICLR 2026) diagnosed *reward hacking* in this recipe, coherence rewards driving collapse to repetitive, low-coverage chords, and added a co-evolving discriminator separating policy trajectories from real data; it traced a Pareto frontier of roughly 85% note-in-chord against Vendi-score diversity, and 12 expert musicians rated it highest on adaptation quality, adaptation speed and perceived control and agency, significantly beating ReaLchords on speed and agency (p < 0.05); the family is now an open PyTorch stack [@wu2026gapt]. Streaming Generation for Music Accompaniment (ISMIR 2026) carried the idea into audio: a decoder-only Transformer over DAC tokens at 50 Hz listens to an incoming stem and emits an accompanying stem across 18 General MIDI instrument families from Slakh2100, formalising two design knobs, *future visibility* (a latency offset from −4 to +4 s) and output chunk size; on 1,024 clips with COCOLA coherence, Beat-F1 and FAD plus blind listening, larger future visibility improved coherence at the cost of required inference speed, and naive maximum-likelihood streaming training proved insufficient without future context [@wu2026streaming; @manilow2019slakh].

The "live music model" line addresses audio at the ensemble level. Magenta RealTime (June 2025) generates a continuous 48 kHz stereo stream in 2-s chunks with a 10-s context over SpectroStream tokens, steered by weighted blends of MusicCoCa text and audio embeddings, trained on roughly 190k hours of instrumental stock music and released as open weights; Lyria RealTime is the hosted sibling with tempo, density and brightness controls and control latency of at most 2 s, whose PromptDJ-MIDI demo maps physical controllers to those parameters [@lyria2025livemusic; @google2025lyriarealtime]. Magenta RealTime 2 (June 2026) cut control latency about 15-fold to roughly 200 ms with 40-ms frames and causal sliding-window attention, runs in real time on Apple Silicon (230M and 2.4B models) with a C++ engine, DAW integration and a suite of example applications built on that engine, and accepts MIDI note input, holding a chord makes the ensemble follow the harmony, with a drums toggle [@lyria2025livemusic]. Live Music Diffusion Models (May 2026) reached similar latency by a different route, repurposing the 340M Stable Audio Open Small into a block-wise streaming generator with KV-caching and "ARC-Forcing" adversarial post-training that curbs error accumulation without RL; it offers text-conditioned streaming, *sketch-based synthesis* from time-aligned top-k CQT loudness controls extracted from the musician's live audio, and stem-conditioned jamming with adjustable future visibility (−2 to +2 s), deployed through ONNX and JUCE as a "generative delay" effect on a gaming laptop at about 30 ms latency; three musicians (saxophone, guitar, cello) described it as a musical partner rather than an effect, with acknowledged EDM bias from MTG-Jamendo training [@novack2026lmdm; @novack2025arc; @evans2024stableaudioopen]. On the symbolic side, MIDInfinite ported the Anticipatory Music Transformer to the browser through WebGPU, generating 51 notes per second on an M3 MacBook Pro and staying ahead of playback 72.9% of the time (86.3% with 2 s of buffering) [@zhou2024midinfinite; @thickstun2024anticipatory]. Aria-Duet placed the 650M Aria piano model in a turn-taking duet on a Disklavier, motivated explicitly by rejecting text prompting as an asynchronous workflow [@bradshaw2025aria].

### 9.4 S5.4 Design spaces, practice and play

The field acquired a systematic map in 2026. A Design Space for Live Music Agents (CHI 2026) reviewed 184 systems (153 papers and 31 videos from 731 screened, 89.8% inter-annotator agreement) into 31 dimensions and 165 codes across four aspects: Usage Context (user and agent roles, topology), Interaction (I/O modality, planning, temporal structure, control mode and scope, system initiative, agency framing as tool, partner or hybrid), Technology (model class, learning, adaptation, latency emphasis, integration) and Ecosystem (authorship, economics, culture); it found a trend from reactive accompanists toward proactive partners, recurring control-versus-novelty and coherence-versus-diversity trade-offs, and that only about 5% of systems discuss ecosystem or policy, and it is published as a living annotated database [@kim2026designspace]. Agents in Concert (IUI 2026) is a reflective case study of deploying multiple agents in a real concert with Rudess and the MIT Chamber Chorus, documenting engineering, rehearsal and performer-agency lessons; its full text was not retrieved and its content is inferred from the related jam_bot coverage [@brade2026agentsinconcert; @blanchard2025jambot]. Both sit within MIT's Music Technology and Computation programme and Huang's research group, whose stated method is weekly co-design with musicians [@mit2026mtcshowcase; @huang2025creativityinteraction]. Behind them lies Rowe's 1993 framework, still the standard vocabulary: score-driven versus performance-driven input, transformative versus generative versus sequenced response, and the instrument paradigm (extending a performer's gesture) versus the player paradigm (an artificial musician with its own personality) [@rowe1993interactive]. The systems in S5.1 are score-driven and in the instrument paradigm; Voyager, OMax and jam_bot are performance-driven players; ReaLchords and Magenta RealTime 2 are performance-driven but transformative, following the human's line.

The practice side is anchored in games and pedagogy. Harmonix, founded in 1995 by Egozy and Rigopulos out of Machover's Media Lab group, whose director's stated goal was "making every human being into a musician," let non-musicians *perform* music through simplified controllers and a scrolling note highway in Guitar Hero (2005) and Rock Band (2007), reportedly selling more than 35 million units [@egozy2005harmonix; @machover1992hyperinstruments]. Egozy's own account describes how these games designed for musical expression within game constraints through scoring, freestyle sections and the note-highway abstraction [@egozy2016harmonix]. Kiri Miller's ethnography argued that such games constitute a form of musical performance and informal learning of rhythm, structure and listening while analysing their limits, and remains the canonical reference for game-based music learning [@miller2009schizophonic]. Harmonix's DropMix (2017) and Fuser (2020) reportedly turned *arrangement by stem selection* into a game mechanic, time-stretching and pitch-shifting stems from different songs to a common key and tempo and scoring drops on downbeats, evidence that casual users assemble coherent arrangements when the system handles alignment [@harmonix2020fuser]. The note highway is the direct visual ancestor of ReaLJam's waterfall and Piano Genie's contour mapping [@scarlatos2025realjam; @donahue2019pianogenie]. Egozy's MIT course 21M.385 Interactive Music Systems has students build real-time Python/Kivy systems, synthesis, sequencing, MIDI and gesture input, note-highway and rhythm-game interfaces, culminating in a public demo concert, a tested curriculum for teaching composers to build musical software [@egozy21m385]. Two CMU systems extend "generate to fit what is playing" beyond composition: Music-aware Virtual Assistants (UIST 2024) re-sang spoken notifications in harmony with the current music and were rated a better fit, less intrusive and more delightful than standard alerts, and RISE (ISMIR 2025) re-sequenced existing songs in real time to match workout intensity through structure analysis and recurrence-matrix cutpoints, with transitions rated 4.3/5 against 4.5/5 for unmodified music by six raters on 514 clips and preferred by 11 of 12 users [@wang2024musicaware; @wang2025rise]. RISE in particular is non-destructive, structure-aware rearrangement of audio, a Play-time analogue of editing form.

### 9.5 Implications for the studio

Play is not a separate product but the same compiled material under a latency budget, and the literature specifies that budget. ReaLJam achieved under 100 ms round trip for symbolic chords, Magenta RealTime 2 about 200 ms control latency for audio on a laptop, Live Music Diffusion Models about 30 ms as a plugin, and Streaming Accompaniment made explicit that coherence rises with future visibility while inference must run faster to pay for it [@scarlatos2025realjam; @lyria2025livemusic; @novack2026lmdm; @wu2026streaming]. The arrangement the studio compiles should therefore be *playable* in the two modes Rowe's taxonomy names: a score-driven instrument mode in which the composer plays or sings against the compiled score and the system follows, the Dannenberg–Vercoe line now available in a browser through online time warping, and a performance-driven player mode in which a ReaLchords-class agent or a Magenta RealTime 2 ensemble responds to what the composer plays on the Nord Stage [@rowe1993interactive; @dannenberg1984online; @egozy2016harmonix; @wu2024realchords]. The ReaLchords recipe, MLE pretraining on lead-sheet data, RL against coherence rewards, distillation from an offline teacher that sees the future, and GAPT's discriminator against collapse, is open in PyTorch and its chord-symbol output plugs directly into a lead-sheet workflow; jam_bot shows that the same class of model can be fine-tuned on one composer's own material and assigned roles [@wu2026gapt; @blanchard2025jambot].

The interaction lessons transfer beyond real time. Every successful system in this section externalises the agent's intent before it commits, ReaLJam's committed-versus-tentative waterfall, jam_bot's preview screen and kill-switch, Notochord's per-sub-event constraints, and the studies show why: adaptation was rated only 2.7/5 even when enjoyment was 4.3/5, five of six musicians wanted to see upcoming chords, and perceived control and agency were the measures on which GAPT most clearly won [@scarlatos2025realjam; @blanchard2025jambot; @shepardson2022notochord; @wu2026gapt]. This is the AI→human communication direction that COFI found almost absent across co-creative systems (S4.4), and the studio's compile step should adopt the same visual grammar for previewing what it is about to change [@rezwana2023cofi]. ReaLJam's finding that protocol preferences varied strongly by individual and did not track objective ratings argues for per-composer settings rather than a single tuned agent, and the Design Space's trade-offs, control versus novelty and coherence versus diversity, are the same axes the offline compiler must expose as annotations [@kim2026designspace].

The gaps are visible in the node counts. Score following is the thinnest node in Play, with no recent system that follows a *compiled, annotated* score and re-synchronises an AI ensemble to a composer's rubato, even though active scores anticipated exactly that representation [@dannenberg2014hcmp]. The RL-tuned agents produce chord symbols rather than arrangements, ignore four-bar phrase structure and lack genre control; the live audio models have no long-form structure and, in the open case, no vocals; and the evidence base is small (N = 6, 12 and 3 interactive participants across the three interactive studies, plus a ten-musician listening panel for ReaLchords) and drawn almost entirely from expert improvisers [@scarlatos2025realjam; @wu2026gapt; @novack2026lmdm; @wu2024realchords]. Only about 5% of live-agent systems discuss authorship or economics, which matters for a studio whose Play mode will generate material a composer may later keep [@kim2026designspace]. The games lineage suggests the remedy on the practice side: Harmonix, Miller and Fuser show that system-handled alignment and clear anticipatory visuals let non-experts perform and arrange with agency, which is the population the studio's Play mode should be evaluated with next [@egozy2005harmonix; @miller2009schizophonic; @harmonix2020fuser].
