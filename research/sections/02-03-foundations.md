## 2. Historical lineages (F1–F3)

The studio's central metaphor — a composer authors material and annotations, and an AI *compiles* a fuller realization that the composer then edits — has three ancestries, each contributing something the others lack. Algorithmic composition (F1) supplied generation from rules and probabilistic style models, and later the idea that a neural model can be a primitive inside a tool rather than an autonomous author. Interactive music systems (F2) supplied real-time listening, score following and Rowe's three axes, which this review's taxonomy reuses directly. The commercial arranger and notation lineage (F3) supplied the only mass-market instantiation of "chords plus style yields an arrangement," a 35-year history whose strengths and limits define what a learned, annotation-steerable descendant must improve upon.

### 2.1 F1 Algorithmic composition lineage

Both paradigms a compiler needs were present at the origin. Hiller and Isaacson's *Illiac Suite* (composed 1956–57 on ILLIAC I) is generally credited as the first computer-composed score; its four experiments ran from cantus firmus generation, through four-voice writing under strict counterpoint rules by generate-and-test, to rhythm and dynamics, and finally to Markov-chain generation, with the symbolic output engraved by hand and performed by a string quartet [@hiller1959experimental]. Experiment 2 (hard constraints) and experiment 4 (soft stylistic priors) are exactly the two modes a compile step must combine. Koenig's Project 1 (1964) and Project 2 (1966), as commonly dated, moved toward the composer programming the generator: selection principles and constraints went in, and a structured table of pitch, duration, register, dynamics and entry delays came out for transcription into notation [@koenig1970project]. Xenakis's UPIC (1977) made drawing an executable score — arcs on a tablet with time on one axis and pitch on the other, rendered directly to sound and transformable by stretching, transposition and inversion — a lineage continued by IanniX and UPISketch [@xenakis1992formalized]. The paradigms are surveyed by @nierhaus2009algorithmic.

The recombinant strand culminated in Cope's Experiments in Musical Intelligence (from 1981): roughly 20,000 lines of Lisp segmented a single composer's corpus, matched "signatures," classified segments with the SPEAC scheme and recombined them under grammatical constraints into new works in the style of Bach, Mozart or Chopin [@cope2001virtual]. Hofstadter's touring "Chopin versus EMI" tests found audiences guessing at roughly chance, *Virtual Music* (2001) collected the ensuing debate on whether pastiche is creativity, and Cope deleted the database in 2005 [@cope2001virtual]. The 1994 CD *Bach by Design* was the first commercial recording of EMI output; the same year McIntyre's "Bach in a Box" harmonised a melody in four-part Baroque style with a genetic algorithm and rule-based fitness — recorded in the notes from recollection rather than a fetched source [@mcintyre1994bachinabox]. EMI's legacy is the canonical warning about style pastiche and authorship, and the origin of the "creativity versus perception of creativity" argument later formalised by Colton (section 3.1).

The neural turn began with Eck and Schmidhuber's 2002 LSTM, which learned 12-bar blues progressions and melodies and is generally credited with keeping global form where earlier recurrent networks drifted [@eck2002blues]. Boulanger-Lewandowski et al.'s RNN-RBM (2012) modelled polyphonic piano-rolls and reportedly popularised the JSB Chorales, MuseData, Nottingham and Piano-midi.de benchmarks that anchored evaluation for a decade [@boulangerlewandowski2012modeling]. folk-rnn (2016) trained an LSTM on roughly 23,000 ABC-notation folk tunes, let musicians steer by key, metre and seed, and was evaluated at population and individual levels and by composers *using* the output, which entered sessions, concerts and an album [@sturm2016folkrnn]; the follow-up case study argued that generated tunes are raw material for editing and that likelihood is the wrong yardstick [@sturm2019machinefolk]. Two Magenta models mark the pivot from generation-as-artifact to generation-as-primitive. Coconet (2017) trained an orderless model over four-voice chorales so that any subset of notes could be masked and re-sampled by blocked Gibbs sampling — "an analogue to rewriting," motivated by composers who "write music in a nonlinear fashion, scribbling motifs here and there" [@huang2017coconet]. Music Transformer (2018–19) made relative self-attention linear in memory, generated minute-long performance MIDI with repeated motifs and ABA-like structure, and demonstrated unconditioned generation, motif continuation and melody-to-accompaniment, with listening studies preferring it to PerformanceRNN [@huang2018musictransformer]. What made these the pivot is what was built on them: Coconet became the Bach Doodle, which answered more than 55 million harmonisation queries in three days and released 21.6 million rated melody–harmonisation pairs [@huang2019bachdoodle], and the Cococo steering interface [@louie2020cococo]; MusicVAE's latent space became an interpolation primitive [@roberts2018musicvae] packaged as clip-level DAW devices in Magenta Studio [@roberts2019magentastudio]. From here the model is a component of a composer's tool; descendants are treated under S3.1–S3.4.

### 2.2 F2 Interactive music systems and accompaniment lineage

Real-time accompaniment has a birth year. At ICMC 1984 Dannenberg matched a live monophonic performance against a stored score by dynamic programming and drove synchronised accompaniment from a tempo estimate [@dannenberg1984online]; at the same conference Vercoe's Synthetic Performer reportedly tracked a flautist through pitch and fingering sensors and learned tempo tendencies across rehearsals [@vercoe1984syntheticperformer]. The Dannenberg line continued through polyphonic following, vocal tracking and the CACM 2006 review, then broadened into Human-Computer Music Performance (2011–14), which cast the computer as a band member with beat- and section-level synchronisation, human cueing and "active scores" — machine-readable, structure-aware scores with repeats and cues that can be re-sequenced live and followed by humans and machines alike [@dannenberg2014hcmp]. Both founders also left a music-as-code inheritance: Vercoe's Csound (1985–86) compiles an orchestra file and a score file into sound [@vercoe1984syntheticperformer], and Dannenberg's Nyquist treats scores as programs of behaviours and temporal combinators, later becoming Audacity's scripting language [@dannenberg1997nyquist].

Rowe's *Interactive Music Systems* (1993) turned this practice into a taxonomy whose axes the present review reuses. Rowe defined an interactive system as a sensing → processing → response chain and classified it on three dimensions [@rowe1993interactive]. Input: *score-driven* systems match incoming events against a stored score and can therefore use beat and metre, whereas *performance-driven* systems have no stored score and rely on perceptual measures such as density and regularity. Response: *transformative* systems vary the material they receive, *generative* systems produce complete output from elementary seeds under rules, and *sequenced* systems play back prerecorded fragments. Relationship: in the *instrument* paradigm the system extends a performer's gesture (solo-like), while in the *player* paradigm it is an artificial musician with its own personality (duet-like). The vocabulary became standard at ICMC and NIME [@rowe1993interactive]. In these terms the studio is a score-driven, transformative system in the instrument paradigm, with optional player agents in Play (S5); the taxonomy's initiative dimension D5 inherits the axes directly.

The player pole is populated by a distinguished line. Lewis's *Voyager* (begun 1987) listens to an improviser through pitch-to-MIDI and generates multiple independent voices that may respond, ignore or initiate — explicitly *not* an instrument the human controls, rooted in Afrological improvisation aesthetics [@lewis2000toomanynotes]. Biles's GenJam (1994) evolved jazz licks over a chord progression with a human "mentor" supplying real-time good/bad judgements as fitness, exposing the fitness bottleneck and formalising *annotation as fitness*, the primitive form of the studio's annotate-and-regenerate loop [@biles1994genjam]. Pachet's Continuator (2003) learned a variable-order Markov model of a player's phrases in real time and continued them in the same style, biasable by external constraints [@pachet2003continuator]. Flow Machines (2012–17) generalised this into *Markov constraints* — style plus hard constraints on metre, harmony and position — and FlowComposer, where users chose a corpus from more than 12,000 lead sheets, entered partial melody or chords, regenerated any selected region under sliders, and in professional use rarely generated more than eight bars at a time [@papadopoulos2016flowcomposer]. IRCAM's OMax and Somax2 learned a Factor Oracle over the live stream and improvised by recombining the performer's own material, without any external corpus [@assayag2006omax]. A parallel MIT strand is institutional: Machover's hyperinstruments (from 1986) augmented instruments with sensors so gesture shaped sound beyond the acoustic instrument, then reached non-professionals and children under the goal of "making every human being into a musician" [@machover1992hyperinstruments]; the sketch-based Hyperscore from that group is treated under S1.3 [@farbood2004hyperscore]. The thread from Vercoe's 1984 accompanist to the RL-tuned ReaLchords of 2024 is forty years long at one institution [@vercoe1984syntheticperformer; @wu2024realchords].

### 2.3 F3 Commercial auto-accompaniment and notation lineage

Band-in-a-Box (PG Music, 1990) is the longest-lived commercial "compile from a lead sheet": chord symbols typed into a bar grid plus key, tempo and style yield a full band arrangement, originally as MIDI styles and since 2007 as RealTracks — recorded human phrases time-stretched, pitch-shifted and spliced to fit the chords [@pgmusic1990bandinabox]. Soloist (1997), RealDrums (2006) and the Audio Chord Wizard (2007) followed; the 2026 release advertises more than 4,400 hours of recordings with 202 new sets, a notation view, DAW export and an "AI-Notes" polyphonic transcriber [@pgmusic2026biab]. The Korg i3 (1993) established the hardware "pro arranger": 48 styles each with four variations, two fills and two intros/endings; left-hand chord recognition driving drums, bass and three to four accompaniment parts in real time; and a 16-track sequencer plus a "Backing Sequencer" that recorded the style performance into an editable song, at about $2,500 [@korg1993i3]. It is credited with inspiring the Roland G-800 and Yamaha PSR variants, and the lineage runs to the Korg Pa5X (2022) and Yamaha Genos2 (2023), which add hundreds of styles, style-creator editors, chord sequencers and style parts on fixed MIDI channels [@korg1993i3].

Notation editors and sequencers form two families that never merged: engraving-first Finale (1988), Encore (1990), Sibelius (1993; Avid since 2006), MuseScore (2002) and Dorico (2016), versus timeline-first Cakewalk (1987), Cubase (1989) and Logic (Notator 1988 → Apple 2002), bridged by MusicXML (2004); the sequencer dates are partially verified [@makemusic2024finale]. MakeMusic discontinued Finale on 26 August 2024, stating that its "massive codebase — millions of lines accumulated over decades — has exponentially increased the challenge of delivering customer value," and offered Finale owners a $149 crossgrade to Dorico Pro (list $579) [@makemusic2024finale]. Dorico 6 (2025) added a rule-based Proofreading panel and no learned features [@steinberg2025dorico6], Sibelius's February 2026 release shipped no AI [@avid2026sibelius], and MuseScore Studio 4.6–4.7 added a beta audio-to-score converter with a 5.0 roadmap toward DAW-like features [@musegroup2025musescore46; @musescore2026studio]. No notation incumbent ships a generative composition assistant in 2026.

The arranger was instead reborn inside the DAW. Logic Pro 11 (May 2024) extended the 2013 Drummer into Session Players: a Bass Player with eight players, complexity and intensity controls and articulations (slides, mutes, dead notes, pickup hits) and a Keyboard Player with four styles, both following the Chord Track, "trained in collaboration with" session musicians and emitting editable MIDI regions [@apple2024logicpro11]. Scaler 3's Arrange page and the Orb and Captain Chords families drive part generators from one shared progression using curated content and a "re-roll a block" convention [@scaler2025v3; @hexachords2022orb], while Ableton Live 12 is the deliberate foil, with rule-based MIDI Generators and machine learning reserved for sample retrieval [@ableton2024live12]. The first learned member on a *commercial* notation surface is Hookpad Aria (2024), DeepBach's 2017 MuseScore plugin having preceded it in research (see S3.2), which infills, continues and harmonises inside a lead-sheet editor and reported a roughly 23% acceptance rate across its deployment (see E3) [@donahue2024hookpadaria].

Together these products give "compile from chords + style" a 35-year history and a stable set of virtues: instant, deterministic, reproducible arrangements; few and legible parameters; editable MIDI output; and, in the best cases, curated, licensed performance data rather than generic synthesis [@pgmusic2026biab; @apple2024logicpro11]. Their limits are equally stable. The control vocabulary is coarse and closed — a style name and a few switches, with no way to say "like this recording" or "quieter here." Styles are fixed pattern libraries external to the composer's material, the source of the lineage's genericness; nothing learns from what the composer wrote. The only input is a chord sequence; audio examples, humming, sketches and text play no role. Output is MIDI or audio rather than notation, the round-trip to a score is weak, and editing one part does not reconcile the others. Structure stops at intro, variation, fill and ending, and no system explains what it did.

### 2.4 Implications for the studio

The lineages agree on the shape of the compile step without sharing a vocabulary. From F1 the studio inherits the two-mode structure of hard constraints and soft stylistic priors, and from Koenig, Xenakis, Csound and Nyquist the idea that a composition is a program whose constraints the composer authors and whose realisation is compiled [@hiller1959experimental; @koenig1970project; @xenakis1992formalized; @vercoe1984syntheticperformer; @dannenberg1997nyquist]. From the Magenta pivot it inherits the finding that the useful unit is a model that fills holes in a human's material in any order, deployable at web scale on a notation surface [@huang2017coconet; @huang2019bachdoodle]. From F2 it inherits Rowe's axes as design coordinates, Dannenberg's active scores as an early machine-followable structured score, Biles's annotation-as-fitness, Pachet's Markov constraints as the pre-deep-learning form of "in my style, subject to my annotations," and OMax's proof that an agent can work from the composer's own material alone [@rowe1993interactive; @dannenberg2014hcmp; @biles1994genjam; @pachet2003continuator; @assayag2006omax]. From F3 it inherits a validated interaction and a market in which the notation incumbents have left generative assistance untouched while the DAWs have rebuilt the arranger with learned players [@apple2024logicpro11; @steinberg2025dorico6; @avid2026sibelius].

The gaps are as instructive. No commercial system accepts the loose modalities the studio proposes or learns from the composer's own material; the research precedents that did — FlowComposer's corpus selection, OMax's reinjection, the Continuator's real-time style learning — never reached the arranger market [@papadopoulos2016flowcomposer; @assayag2006omax; @pachet2003continuator]. The engraving and sequencing families remain split, so a notation-first compiler that renders to audio has no incumbent architecture to copy, and the Finale exit shows that legacy monoliths are liabilities and an open engraving core the safer foundation [@makemusic2024finale; @musegroup2025musescore46]. And the lineage leaves the AI silent: from the Illiac Suite to Session Players, no system explains which constraint or style choice produced which note — a gap section 3 shows to be general. The history therefore argues that the studio should present its compile step as the learned, annotation-steered successor of the arranger — keeping instant, deterministic, editable output and legible parameters — while replacing the closed style vocabulary with the composer's material and annotations, treating notation as the source of truth, and adding reconciliation across parts, structure beyond the fill, and an account of what was done. Aria's telemetry suggests that even the lead-sheet version of this proposition finds users [@donahue2024hookpadaria]; the arranger keyboard shows what the full-arrangement version could feel like [@korg1993i3].

## 3. Theories of human–AI co-creation (F4–F5)

Where section 2 traced what has been built, this section reviews how human–computer creative partnership has been theorised and evaluated, and which research programmes share the studio's stance. The frameworks (F4) supply the vocabulary in which the studio's position is stated — initiative, task division, communication direction, the AI's role — and the instruments by which its claims can be tested. The programmes and design spaces (F5) show that "steerable, decomposable, interactive rather than imitative" is a shared agenda at Magenta and MIT, CMU, Sony CSL and the Metacreation Lab.

### 3.1 F4 Theories of co-creativity and creativity support

The creativity-support-tools agenda gave the field its first principles and its standard instrument. Shneiderman's 2007 essay argued that creativity support tools should support exploratory search and collaboration, provide rich history-keeping (undo, versioning, replay), offer low thresholds with high ceilings and wide walls, allow many paths and styles, and be evaluated by long-term case studies rather than short lab tasks [@shneiderman2007cst]. Cherry and Latulipe's Creativity Support Index (2014) is a validated questionnaire with six factors — Exploration, Expressiveness, Immersion, Enjoyment, Results Worth Effort and Collaboration — weighted by paired-factor comparisons to yield a 0–100 score; it has become the default instrument in music co-creation studies, used by Cococo, MMM-C and Amuse [@cherry2014csi; @louie2020cococo; @tchemeube2023mmmc; @kim2025amuse]. Shneiderman's human-centered AI framework replaced the one-dimensional trade-off between automation and control with a two-dimensional space and argued for the high-control, high-automation quadrant — "supertools" rather than autonomous agents — with audit trails and explainability [@shneiderman2020hcai]. The studio's thesis that a compiler can be highly automated and highly controlled at once is, in this framework, a design target rather than a paradox.

Mixed-initiative and role frameworks describe who does what, when. The Mixed-Initiative Creative Interfaces workshop (CHI 2017) placed such systems between human-driven tools and autonomous computational creativity, defined them by a tight loop of constraining, suggesting and evaluating, and named the open challenges: letting non-programmers express formal constraints, legibility of AI decisions, fatigue, evaluation of co-creativity, and de-skilling [@deterding2017mici]. Annotations are precisely a means for non-programmers to express constraints, so these are the studio's research questions. Lubart's roles — *nanny* (managing process), *pen-pal* (mediating human collaboration), *coach* (teaching techniques) and *colleague* (generating alternatives the human evaluates and refines) — remain the most-cited role vocabulary [@lubart2005partners]. Kantosalo and Toivonen, working from Wiggins's creativity-as-search formalism, distinguished *alternating* co-creativity (turn-taking on one artefact) from *task-divided* co-creativity (agents own different sub-tasks in parallel) [@kantosalo2016modes]. Role preference has proved individual: Guzdial et al.'s turn-based Morai Maker level editor, across studies with more than 100 participants (91 in the controlled study), found designers split between wanting a friend, collaborator, student or manager, with the same agent rated helpful or annoying accordingly [@guzdial2019friend]. Davis et al.'s Drawing Apprentice introduced participatory sense-making for turn-by-turn analysis and reported that users often could not distinguish the agent from a Wizard-of-Oz human [@davis2016drawingapprentice]. Wan et al.'s prewriting study with GPT-3 (N=15) proposed LLM-led ideation followed by human-led illumination and implementation, with humans holding initiative except when blocked [@wan2024secondmind].

Rezwana and Maher's COFI is the most complete interaction vocabulary and the source of the field's most consequential negative finding. It decomposes co-creation into *collaboration* dimensions — participation style (alternating or parallel), timing, task distribution, contribution type, creative process — and *communication* dimensions split into human-to-AI channels (direct manipulation, embodied, intentional) and AI-to-human channels [@rezwana2023cofi]. Coding 92 systems, the authors found the field concentrated on generative ability and almost entirely lacked AI-to-human communication — explanations, intentions, confidence — which they argued distinguishes a partner from a tool [@rezwana2023cofi]. Their Creative Penpal study compared a sketching partner that only drew with one that also communicated its intentions; the communicating version was reportedly perceived as more collaborative, engaging and reliable, though some found it intrusive [@rezwana2022perceptions]. The gap is visible in music: Cococo's users developed "debugging" strategies to infer what the model was doing [@louie2020cococo], and the systems that do communicate back — RefinPaint's critic marking *where* to modify, ReaLJam's committed-versus-tentative chord waterfall, the XAI-for-the-arts programme's visualised latent dimensions — are recent exceptions treated under S4.4 [@ramoneda2024refinpaint; @scarlatos2025realjam; @bryankinns2021xai].

Interactive machine learning supplies the theory of example-based specification. Fiebrink and Caramiaux argued that a learning algorithm should be understood as an interface, and that learning by demonstration lets a musician teach an idiosyncratic mapping in minutes and then "break the rules"; the Wekinator's record–train–play–correct loop showed over years of workshops that examples and fast iteration beat parameter tweaking for non-programmers [@fiebrink2018mlcreativetool]. Scurto et al.'s Co-Explorer extended this to feedback-as-steering, with a reinforcement-learning agent adapting to like/dislike judgements in a synthesiser's parameter space [@scurto2021coexplorer], and Piano Genie, which decodes eight buttons into piano music so the human supplies contour and rhythm while the model supplies pitches, is the archetype of *AI as instrument* [@donahue2019pianogenie].

Computational-creativity theory supplies the terms in which creativity can be evaluated at all. Boden's distinction between combinational, exploratory and transformational creativity lets the studio say that its compiler performs exploratory generation inside a composer-defined space while the human supplies the transformational moves [@boden2004creativemind]. Ritchie's eighteen criteria over an "inspiring set," using typicality and quality ratings, ask whether output is both typical of the source material and good without copying it [@ritchie2007criteria]. Colton's tripod — skill, appreciation, imagination — stressed perception and framing, and *appreciation*, the ability to judge one's own output against the brief, is the leg most generators lack [@colton2008tripod]. Jordanous's SPECS defines creativity for the domain from fourteen components, chooses standards and tests against them, with a case study on improvisation systems [@jordanous2012standardised]. Karimi et al.'s four questions — *who* evaluates, *what* (product, process, interaction, experience), *when*, *how* — revealed that co-creative systems overwhelmingly evaluate user experience rather than the creativity of the collaboration [@karimi2018evaluating]. These are taken up as instruments in E5; the table records the vocabulary the taxonomy adopts and where the studio sits.

### 3.2 F5 Human-centered music-AI programmes and design spaces

Anna Huang's work is the most sustained articulation of a compatible philosophy, stated in her own words: creativity elicited "not through imitation but through interaction," Coconet's block-Gibbs procedure as support for "a nonlinear compositional process," and the aim "to build systems that musicians can shape, negotiate, and jam with," with visualisations that help them "understand and steer system behavior" [@huang2025creativityinteraction]. The trajectory runs from ChordRipple's chord recommendations at Harvard [@huang2016chordripple] through Magenta — Coconet, Music Transformer, the Bach Doodle and Cococo, whose study (N=21) raised controllability, comprehensibility, ownership and self-efficacy over a plain infilling baseline [@huang2017coconet; @huang2018musictransformer; @huang2019bachdoodle; @louie2020cococo] — to two field-defining studies. The AI Song Contest analysis of 13 teams (61 people) found that practitioners decomposed songwriting into building blocks, generated massively and curated because models were not steerable, and asked for interfaces that are "decomposable, steerable, interpretable, and adaptive" [@huang2020aisongcontest]. Expressive Communication crossed two models with two interfaces (26 composers, 1,020 listener comparisons) and found that better steering interfaces and more expressive models each independently improved composer control, ownership and self-efficacy as well as listener-judged musicality — the two are complementary rather than substitutes [@louie2022expressive]. Since 2024 her group at MIT — where she is Robert N. Noyce Career Development Professor with a shared appointment in EECS and Music and Theater Arts — has turned toward RL-tuned jamming agents — ReaLchords, ReaLJam's intention-displaying waterfall, the jam_bot built with Jordan Rudess, the concert case study Agents in Concert — and weekly co-design with working musicians [@wu2024realchords; @scarlatos2025realjam; @blanchard2025jambot; @brade2026agentsinconcert]. It sits inside MIT's Music Technology and Computation programme, launched Fall 2024 with Egozy as director, which held its first showcase on 13 May 2026 with a founding cohort of five and admitted ten students from more than a hundred applicants for 2026–27 [@mit2026mtcshowcase]; its ancestors are Vercoe's studio and Machover's hyperinstruments, both framing technology as expansion of human expression [@vercoe1984syntheticperformer; @machover1992hyperinstruments].

Chris Donahue's Generative Creativity Lab (G-CLef) at CMU, founded 2023 to "empower and enrich human creativity and productivity with generative AI," is the closest academic analogue of the studio's loop — CMU's music-technology activity otherwise splitting between the School of Music and the Dannenberg line [@cmu2025musictech] [@gclef2026lab]. Its line runs from Piano Genie [@donahue2019pianogenie] through the Anticipatory Music Transformer, whose asynchronous conditioning lets a human pin arbitrary notes while the model fills the rest [@thickstun2024anticipatory], to the deployed Hookpad Aria [@donahue2024hookpadaria], to Amuse (CHI 2025 Best Paper), which turned image, text and audio inspirations into chords via an editable keyword intermediate and rejection sampling against a harmonic prior, improving inspiration support, controllability, collaborative feel, expressiveness and exploration for ten songwriters with no time penalty [@kim2025amuse], and to Decomposer (2026), which decompiles MIDI into executable Strudel programs so that repeats, voices and harmony become editable code — the founder's "composition as program" run in the decompile direction [@kim2026decomposer]. Donahue holds the Dannenberg professorship, and the two CMU generations connect through Dai and Dannenberg's finding that deep generators differ strikingly from human pop in repetition and structure, answered with user-editable structural skeletons [@dai2022missing; @dai2021controllable].

The CHI 2026 *Design Space for Live Music Agents*, a Donahue–Huang collaboration, is the first systematic map of real-time human–AI music collaboration, coding 184 systems into 31 dimensions; it is treated in detail under S5.4, and its dimensions overlap D4–D6 and D8 for the real-time corner [@kim2026designspace].

Sony CSL Paris represents a practitioner-embedded stance with a longer pedigree, from the Continuator and Flow Machines [@pachet2003continuator; @papadopoulos2016flowcomposer] to Deruty et al.'s six- to eighteen-month collaborations with six professional acts using DrumNet, BassNet, DrumGAN and related prototypes. Thematic analysis yielded "pull" over "push," *priming* on the artist's own audio, generate-and-curate and the exploitation of artefacts, alongside the friction of leaving the DAW and the lack of recognisable control; the authors proposed validating tools by workflow integration, task simplification, stimulation, identifiable contribution and commercial viability, and argued that studio pop practice is audio-first — a counterpoint the symbolic-first studio must answer by rendering stems [@deruty2022sonycsl]. RefinPaint, a UPF–Sony CSL Tokyo collaboration, extends the stance to critique [@ramoneda2024refinpaint].

The Metacreation Lab at Simon Fraser has pursued the multi-track symbolic compiler with unusual continuity and measured it. The Multi-Track Music Machine became Calliope, in which users select bars and tracks for infilling, batch-generate up to 1,000 candidates and rank them under per-track controls for instrument, density, polyphony and duration [@tchemeube2022calliope]. Integrated into Cubase as the one-parameter MMM-C and evaluated with 18 expert composers, it scored SUS 71.4–75.7 across three tasks and highest on CSI enjoyment, but ease of control was 5.2/10 while *desire for more control* averaged 9.5/10 — the cost of under-parameterised control even inside the DAW [@tchemeube2023mmmc]. The lineage continued into MMM4Live [@metacreation2023mmm4live] and MIDI-GPT (AAAI 2025), with bar-level multi-track infilling and attribute controls under an Open RAIL-M licence [@pasquier2025midigpt].

A final programme is pedagogical and functions as a design stance. Egozy's 21M.385 Interactive Music Systems at MIT has students build real-time musical software in Python with Kivy and the `imslib` library — synthesis and looping, MIDI sequencing, generative composition, gesture sensing, game design and visualisation — culminating in a public demo concert, with Guitar Hero as the case study of interactive systems' cultural reach [@egozy21m385]. The stance is Harmonix's: Guitar Hero (2005) and Rock Band (2007), co-founded by Egozy and Rigopulos out of Machover's group, let non-musicians perform through simplified controllers and a scrolling note highway, reportedly selling more than 35 million units [@egozy2005harmonix]; Egozy's account of musical expression in those games, and later work on in-browser score following and AI-augmented instruments, articulates how agency survives inside constrained interfaces [@egozy2016harmonix]. Miller's ethnography argued the games are genuine performance and informal learning [@miller2009schizophonic], and Fuser and DropMix showed casual users building coherent arrangements from constrained stems [@harmonix2020fuser]. ConcertCue, streaming time-synchronised programme notes cued to musical events, is a deployed time-aligned annotation system aimed at listeners [@egozy2018concertcue], and Cuthbert's 21M.383 teaches composers to treat notation as data over the music21 corpus — the mindset behind composition as a program [@cuthbert2023ocw21m383; @cuthbert2010music21]. The note highway is the ancestor of ReaLJam's waterfall and Piano Genie's contour mapping [@scarlatos2025realjam; @donahue2019pianogenie], and the pedagogy's insistence on low latency, visual clarity and playfulness is a design prior for Play mode.

### 3.3 Implications for the studio

The frameworks let the studio's position be stated exactly, and the statement exposes where it departs from the literature. In Rowe's terms the compiler is score-driven, transformative and in the instrument paradigm; in Lubart's it is a colleague at compile time and a coach when it annotates back; in Kantosalo and Toivonen's it alternates on the score and divides tasks by role; in Shneiderman's it must occupy the high-control, high-automation quadrant [@rowe1993interactive; @lubart2005partners; @kantosalo2016modes; @shneiderman2020hcai]. In COFI's terms it is human-initiated and bidirectional — and this is the departure, because AI-to-human communication was almost entirely absent across 92 coded systems [@rezwana2023cofi]. Evidence that such communication is wanted comes from Creative Penpal, from Cococo's users reverse-engineering the model, and from ReaLJam's pianists asking to see the agent's plan, while RefinPaint shows what a critic that marks *where* to change looks like [@rezwana2022perceptions; @louie2020cococo; @scarlatos2025realjam; @ramoneda2024refinpaint]. A compiler that returns annotations — what it did with each mark, where it is unsure, what alternatives it considered — is therefore both a theoretical requirement and an empirical gap (S4.4, D6).

The programmes converge on requirements the studio should treat as settled. Practitioners decompose, steer and curate and want interpretability [@huang2020aisongcontest]; steering interfaces move ownership more than stronger models, and the two are complementary [@louie2022expressive]; one control parameter inside the DAW is not enough [@tchemeube2023mmmc]; professionals pull rather than accept pushes, prime on their own material and refuse to leave their tools [@deruty2022sonycsl]; examples beat parameters for musicians [@fiebrink2018mlcreativetool]; initiative must be adjustable because role preference is individual [@guzdial2019friend]; and ownership tracks the strength of the human's authored input [@krol2025ownership]. The MICI challenges remain open and are the questions an annotation layer must answer [@deterding2017mici], while the live-agents design space shows the real-time corner well mapped and ecosystem questions addressed by one system in twenty [@kim2026designspace].

Two gaps remain. First, evaluation: co-creative systems overwhelmingly measure user experience, long-term case studies were called for in 2007, and longitudinal music studies remain rare [@karimi2018evaluating; @shneiderman2007cst; @deruty2022sonycsl]. The studio should adopt the CSI with ownership, control and comprehension items, pair it with listener judgements in the Expressive Communication protocol, apply Ritchie's criteria and SPECS's components to compiled output, and log interventions as provenance from the first prototype (E1, E5) [@cherry2014csi; @louie2022expressive; @ritchie2007criteria; @jordanous2012standardised]. Second, the surface: every programme reviewed here operates on a piano-roll, a lead sheet, a DAW clip or a live instrument; none has articulated a human-centered agenda for the engraved score, the surface on which composers in the classical and arranging traditions actually annotate. The frameworks are ready; the notation-native instance of them is the studio's contribution to make.
